### Alustriel's Arcana

In this book, the archmage Alustriel Silverhand from the Forgotten Realms setting—a human daughter of the god of magic Mystra and paragon of righteousness and virtue—presents arcana she has discovered during her travels. (You can learn more about Alustriel in Alustriel Silverhand)

But the contents of this book can be used in any setting, whether it's the Forgotten Realms, Ravenloft, or one of your own making. Think of this book as a toy box full of options to incorporate into your game as the Dungeon Master chooses.

This book relies on the rules in the Player's Handbook,Dungeon Master's Guide, and Monster Manual.

#### Schools of Magic

The eight schools of magic that underlie spellcraft are key to this book's magical character options, and its magical factions and tools for building magical worlds are tied to the schools' nuanced characteristics. The following entries expand on the schools' brief descriptions in the School of Magic

- **Abjuration** spells include protective and healing magic, as well as magic that prevents or reverses unwanted effects.
- **Conjuration** spells involve creating objects and effects, summoning creatures, or teleporting.
- **Divination** spells allow the caster to peer into the future or other planes of existence to gain helpful or bolstering benefits or information.
- **Enchantment** spells include magic that bends targets' minds to the caster's will.
- **Evocation** spells create physical effects that are often spectacular and might include destructive energy such as fire, cold, and acid.
- **Illusion** spells include magic that creates sounds, images, smells, and other sensory effects that deceive the mind or senses.
- **Necromancy** spells manipulate life and death, including restoring the dead to life, animating undead creatures, and siphoning life from the living.
- **Transmutation** spells include magic that transforms creatures and objects, granting characteristics and abilities they previously lacked.

> **The Weave of Magic**
> The worlds within the D&D multiverse are magical places. All existence is suffused with magical power, and potential energy lies untapped in every rock, stream, and living creature—even in the air itself. Raw magic is present in every manifestation of energy throughout the multiverse.
> Mortals can't directly shape this raw magic. Instead, they use a fabric of magic—an interface between the will of a spellcaster and raw magic. The spellcasters of the Forgotten Realms call it the Weave and recognize its essence as the god Mystra, but casters have varied ways of naming and conceptualizing this interface.
> Whenever a magic effect is created, the threads of the Weave intertwine, twist, and fold to make the effect possible. A spellcaster surrounded by the Weave can shape lightning to blast foes, transport hundreds of miles in the blink of an eye, or even reverse death.
> But in places where the Weave is damaged, magic works in unpredictable ways—and if it's torn, raw magic is inaccessible. Even the most powerful archmage can't light a candle via magic in an area where the Weave has been torn.

> > My Esteemed Heroes,
> > Magic thrums through our multiverse. Powerful magic can save or destroy worlds, though magic's balance is as delicate as a thread.
> > For centuries, as a daughter of the goddess of magic Mystra, I have protected the Weave and fostered the good in all creatures, lest wickedness snap the Weave.
> > This has happened more than once, and the resulting suffering was immense. To prevent the unthinkable, magic must cultivate love, joy, and hope and banish hatred and pain. While I draw breath, I shall pursue this cause.
> > My friends—heroes—I hope you will join me.
> > However, I know you can't join my cause without knowledge of magic's greatest wonders. And so I bequeath in this tome arcana I have collected throughout my long life.
> > I hope you use this arcana to better your worlds, but I can't make that choice for you. I simply reveal the gifts of my divine mother and trust you will use them to do good.
> > May Mystra smile on you always,
> > — Alustriel Silverhand

#### Origin Feats

These feats are in the Origin category.

##### Arcane Artist

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Minor Illusion cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Inspiring Magic

When you cast a spell from the Illusion school, you can choose one ally within 30 feet of yourself who can see you. That ally gains Heroic Inspiration. Once you use this benefit, you can't use it again until you finish a Long Rest.

##### Arcane Eloquence

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Vicious Mockery cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Smooth Talker

When you make a Charisma (Deception, Intimidation, or Persuasion) check, you can roll 1d4 and add the number rolled to the ability check.

##### Arcane Infiltrator

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Friends cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Cunning Diversion

You can take the Dodge action as a Bonus Action. You can use this benefit a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

##### Arcane Omens

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Guidance cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Helpful Premonition

When you or a creature you can see within 30 feet of yourself fails a saving throw, you can take a Reaction to roll 1d4 and add the number rolled to the save's total, potentially turning the failure into a success. You can use this benefit a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

##### Arcane Overload

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Fire Bolt cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Power Surge

When you cast an Evocation spell and deal damage with it, you can add your Proficiency Bonus to one damage roll of that spell. Once you use this benefit, you can't do so again until you finish a Long Rest.

##### Arcane Safeguard

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn the Resistance cantrip. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat). You can cast the Resistance cantrip as a Bonus Action. You can do so a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

###### Sheltering Aid

When you take the Help action to assist with an ally's ability check, that ally gains a number of Temporary Hit Points equal to your Proficiency Bonus.

##### Arcane Undertaker

You gain the following benefits.

###### Cantrip

You learn one Cleric or Wizard cantrip of your choice. The cantrip must be from the Necromancy school. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat).

###### Knowledge from the Dead

When you make an Intelligence (History) or Wisdom (Medicine) check, you can roll 1d4 and add the number rolled to the ability check.

###### Understanding of Death

When you take the Help action to stabilize a creature with 0 Hit Points, you gain Heroic Inspiration. Once you use this benefit, you can't use it again until you finish a Long Rest.

##### Familiar Friend

You gain the following benefits.

###### Faithful Companion

You always have the Find Familiar spell prepared. Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma is your spellcasting ability for this spell (choose when you select this feat). You can cast it once without a spell slot or Material components, and you regain the ability to cast it in this way when you finish a Long Rest. You can also cast the spell using any spell slots you have.

###### Fortified Familiar

When you cast the Find Familiar spell, your familiar's Hit Point maximum and current Hit Points are increased by an amount equal to twice your character level.

###### Helpful Friend

When you make an ability check using a skill in which you have proficiency while your familiar is within 5 feet of you, you gain Advantage on the check. You can use this benefit a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

##### Portal Jumper

You gain the following benefits.

###### Otherworldly Resilience

You have Resistance to one of the following damage types: Necrotic, Psychic, or Radiant (choose when you select this feat).

###### Portal Step

You can spend 15 feet of movement to teleport to an unoccupied space you can see within 15 feet of yourself. You can use this benefit a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus but only once per turn, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

##### Transmuted Anatomy

You gain the following benefits.

###### Lengthened Stride

Your Speed increases by 5 feet.

###### Resilient Anatomy

You have Advantage on saving throws against effects that would compel you to shape-shift against your will. Additionally, when you fail a Constitution saving throw, you can take a Reaction to roll 1d4 and add the number rolled to the save, potentially turning the failure into a success. You can use this benefit a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

You can't have more than one familiar at a time. If you cast this spell while you already have a familiar from this spell, that familiar is replaced by the new one.

## Chapter 3: Creating Magical Worlds

*[Image: In highly magical worlds, people use magic for major tasks such as construction. (book/AU/028-03-001.ch-3-splash.webp)]*

Using the advice and tools in this chapter, you can create your own world infused with magic for your D&D games. This chapter also provides guidelines for tailoring a group patron for your characters' adventures, helping the players feel immersed in your setting. When creating your magical world, you can choose the options you like best or modify an existing world using your favorite advice. For example, you could use an archmage group patron of your own creation in your adventures that take place in the Forgotten Realms.

### Magic in Existing Worlds

If you want to use this chapter's advice within an existing world, consider the ones on the D&D Settings table. It describes the magic prevalent in each setting as well as where you can find more information about those settings.

### Building a Magical World

The advice presented here will help you build your own magical world. Each of the sections that follow focuses on a different aspect of world-building, beginning with the broadest concepts and proceeding to specific topics, including questions designed to guide your creativity. #### Outline Your World

*[Image: Performers infuse their music with magic to create a joyous environment. (book/AU/029-03-002.magic-performers.webp)]*

Before you create individual realms and societies, determine basic details about magic in your world. Consider which categories of magic are most or least prevalent, how that magic shaped the world's geography, and the nature of the major realms.

##### Categories of Magic

In the worlds of D&D, magic can be categorized in three main ways: by different approaches (arcane or divine), by the schools of magic, and by distinct magical traditions. You might use any of these categories to define the role of magic in your world.

###### Approaches to Magic

The spells of Bards, Sorcerers, Warlocks, and Wizards are commonly called arcane magic. These practitioners rely on direct apprehension of the laws and principles of magic. Wizards study those principles directly, Sorcerers and Bards have a more intuitive understanding, and Warlocks gain that knowledge through their pacts.

The spells of Clerics, Druids, Paladins, and Rangers are called divine magic, though the nature magic of Druids and Rangers is sometimes divided into its own category: primal magic. All these practitioners approach magic mediated by gods, the forces of nature, the sacred weight of a Paladin's oath, or some other divine force.

###### Schools of Magic

The eight schools of magic are Abjuration, Conjuration, Divination, Enchantment, Evocation, Illusion, Necromancy, and Transmutation. These schools represent the underpinnings of all magic in a D&D world. All D&D spells belong to one of these schools.

In your world, spellcasters might experience sensory effects when casting spells of different schools. For example, their scalps might tingle while casting Evocation spells, they might see flashes of events in their peripheral vision while casting Divination spells, or they might smell the phantom scent of grave soil while casting Necromancy spells.

###### Magical Traditions

Spellcasters of any sort might practice a wide variety of magical traditions. Dragons claim to be the originators of magic on some worlds. Some magical traditions rely entirely on rituals (which characters can access through the Ritual Caster feat in the Player's Handbook). Rune magic (discussed in Bigby Presents: Glory of the Giants) was pioneered by giants and represents a distinct magical tradition. In the Forgotten Realms setting, ancient elves developed the tradition of high magic and used it to transform the world. Spellcasters in that setting also developed the practice of Circle magic (detailed in Forgotten Realms: Heroes of Faerûn), which allows spellcasters to pool their efforts to cast powerful spells. Magical traditions can also be passed down through family lines, as is the case with Eberron's dragonmarks—magical birthmarks that grant the use of specific spells (as described in Eberron: Forge of the Artificer).

##### Magical Geography

Think about the ways magic has shaped the physical landscape of your world. Perhaps a powerful archmage, desiring privacy, used magic to raise mountains around her home. Or a nation governed by powerful mages might cloak the realm in protective magic to block its enemies' magical prying. Your world might boast enormous monuments or strange geographical features created by magic in eons long gone or left behind by the gods in an ancient war.

Magic can have more modest effects too, which nonetheless impact the daily life of your world's residents. For example, in Calimport in the Forgotten Realms, a magical lake inside the city supplies water for residents, while a floating tower serves as headquarters for soldiers who ride flying carpets. The more common magic is in your setting, the more visible its effects should be throughout the world.

##### Magical Realms

As you think about your world's geography, consider the realms within it. Are they all home to powerful spellcasters? Are some more reliant on magic than others?

Your world might include three realms that each rely on a different type of magic. For example, one realm is ruled by wizards who each focus on a particular school of magic. In contrast, another realm is the domain of warlocks who gain their powers from a mysterious, otherworldly entity, while the third realm is ruled by sorcerers who descend from dragons and those who worshipped them. This emphasis on different kinds of spellcasting in each realm gives each a distinct flavor and culture.

On the other hand, you might find it useful to have realms in your world that don't emphasize magic to serve as a contrast to the realms that do. A small realm surrounded by warlike neighbors with large armies of ordinary soldiers, for example, might turn to magic to defend itself and become a haven for mages.

#### Attitudes Toward Magic

Once you've determined the types of magic that pervade your world, consider how your world's inhabitants view magic. Do they welcome magic and the potential it represents? Or do they resent and fear magic, seeing it as a force for unexpected change and danger? Do attitudes vary by region?

##### Trust in or Distrust of Magic

A society that has relied on magic for generations has likely grown accustomed to magic's effects on daily life. Such societies' traditions and practices take magic into account. If access to the Find Familiar spell is common, for example, public spaces and businesses would be designed to accommodate familiars as well as people.

Alternatively, a society might distrust magic that's not a part of daily life. Perhaps an unfamiliar kind of magic is blamed for a memorable tragedy, leading people to distrust that type of magic. For example, a realm that's been invaded by an army of undead might prohibit necromancy even if the realm encourages other schools of magic.

##### Magical Shortcuts

Magic can often accomplish tasks more quickly and easily than is normally possible. While people in a magic-rich society easily accept the convenience and reliability of magical substitutes for common services, there might be a backlash effect or unusual side effects from folk taking magic for granted.

For example, wealthy nobles might seek out a chef capable of making food "the old-fashioned way." Or a society rife with healing magic might become obsessed with thrill-seeking and dangerous sports.

#### Religion

Most D&D worlds have many gods, and a magical society usually has at least one god of magic.

Are there gods of magic in your world? Who are they? What do they expect of their worshippers and from individuals who wield magic? Are they benevolent, antagonistic, or ambivalent toward mortals?

##### Benevolent Magic Gods

You might include a benevolent god of magic in your world. Such a god believes magic should be used for good, improving the lives of ordinary people. They encourage teaching and promoting the use of magic, and aspiring mages are expected to maintain a moral and ethical code that emphasizes selflessness, duty, and charity.

For example, crafters in a world with a benevolent god of magic are encouraged to make Common magic items that benefit others. When adventurers recover lost magic from ancient dungeons, priests hide dangerous magic away while they share useful magic with as many people as possible.

Including a benevolent god of magic in your world doesn't preclude the inclusion of evil mages. Indeed, a benevolent god of magic might be outnumbered or outmatched by evil rivals. This prompts the god of magic or their followers to seek help from the characters.

###### Mystra

Mystra is a benevolent god of magic in the Forgotten Realms setting. Mystra has been destroyed or defeated multiple times, but each time she has returned in a new form with the help of brave adventurers.

##### Antagonistic Magic Gods

A god of magic might spread evil, cruelty, and tyranny through the use of magic. An antagonistic god of magic favors nefarious uses of magic. Such a god's necromancer followers might raise large armies of undead to unleash on the population. Or adherents might use powerful Evocation spells to destroy rival cities and those who live in them. An antagonistic god of magic might rule a realm dedicated to evil and populated with undead, devils or demons, and other bloodthirsty creatures.

###### Iuz and Vecna

The demigod Iuz, described in the Dungeon Master's Guide, and Vecna, whose schemes to amass ultimate power are described in Vecna: Eve of Ruin, are both antagonistic gods who seek conquest through magic. Iuz is primarily a god of deceit and oppression, but his methods involve seeking ancient magic he can use to increase his power. Vecna plots from the shadows and reveals himself only when victory is nigh.

##### Ambivalent Magic Gods

A god of magic might be ambivalent about their followers. Such a deity cares more about whether magic is used than about how it is used. When characters recover lost magical secrets during an adventure, an ambivalent god of magic might want those magical secrets locked away and preserved—not out of a selfish desire to control the information, but out of an intellectual interest in ensuring this knowledge isn't lost again.

###### Mystryl

An ambivalent god of magic might guard the source of all magic or even embody magic. Mystryl, the original god of magic in the Forgotten Realms, embodied magic in this way. When Mystryl died, magic was transformed; spellcasting became unreliable, and spellcasters lost their ability to cast their most powerful spells. Magic stabilized only when the god of magic returned as Mystra.

###### Boccob

An ambivalent god of magic in the Greyhawk setting and patron of the legendary mage Mordenkainen, Boccob works to ensure that good and evil forces remain equal.

##### Weaving a Divine Tapestry

A high-magic setting might include multiple gods of magic, each with conflicting goals and methods. No single god is likely to completely satisfy a society ruled by mages where spellcasting is common. Including multiple competing gods of magic gives your players more choices for patron gods for their characters. The conflict between rival gods of magic can create adventures and drive campaigns.

Your world might have different gods for each school of magic (a god of Conjuration, for example, and a god of Transmutation). Or a primary god of magic might be assisted by other gods, each with their own emphasis. For example, Mystra is assisted by Azuth, a god of wizardry who is ambivalent in his outlook and uninterested in the moral and ethical use of magic.

Your world might even have no god focused entirely on magic. In a world where magic suffuses every aspect of life, every god might carry some responsibility for the use of magic as it impacts the rest of their portfolio. A god of war cares not just about weapons and bloodshed, but about the use of magic in battle. A god of crafting might have dominion over making magic items and over artisans who use magic in their trades. And a god of knowledge might govern the academic study of magic often associated with gods of magic. Eberron's pantheon is a good example.

#### Technology

Many D&D campaigns assume the technology of a medieval or early renaissance setting. However, the Dungeon Master's Guide has rules for modern firearms and futuristic weapons such as Antimatter Rifles and Laser Pistols. Further examples of futuristic technology can be found in Quests from the Infinite Staircase.

When you create your magical world, consider what level of technology is present. Does advanced technology exist in your setting? If it does, what do people think of it? Is technology a replacement for or a supplement to magic?

##### Technological Revolution

To introduce more advanced technology into your world, you can present it as originating in a recent technological revolution. An ancient and magic-focused realm ruled by mages might suddenly discover technological innovation. This could streamline daily life with simple technology, such as long-range communications, and add options for characters who like technology. It could also threaten the realm's rulers and therefore lead to technology being heavily regulated. Threatened rulers might hunt down inventors and destroy their creations. On the flip side, rulers might feel threatened by magic-users, leading these rulers to embrace technology to stave off power-hungry mages.

##### Rival Powers

*[Image: In Eberron, magic mimics technology, allowing wonders such as airships. (book/AU/030-03-003.eberron-magic.webp)]*

Alternatively, you can introduce technology to your world by creating a rival realm that relies on technology much like your magical society relies on magic. These two realms could be equivalent in wealth, status, and influence, but what one accomplishes with spells, the other achieves through machines.

Consider whether characters might come from either realm, or if the entire party hails from one society or the other. The first case encourages wildly different characters; some heroes enter battle with the Magic Missile and Fireball spells prepared, while others rely on their trusty Laser Pistols and Grenade Launchers.

If all characters come from one of the realms, the other society can serve as a rival and antagonist. If, for example, all your characters are from a magical society bordering a high-technology realm, tension between these two lands runs high and conflict is inevitable. Your campaign might begin with the characters infiltrating their technologically advanced neighbor while concealing their magical skills. There, the characters discover a plan for a mechanical army to invade the party's homeland. To thwart this invasion, the characters need to lead a force of conjured creatures, undead, and other magical warriors.

##### Magic and Technology in Tandem

Magic and technology need not be oppositional forces. You can create a society in which magic and technology operate side by side. In such a realm, bandits are just as likely to cast the Fire Bolt spell as they are to draw a Laser Pistol. Citizens predict the weather with the Druidcraft spell, but they tell time using a clock in the middle of town.

###### Eberron

In a variant of this approach, magic provides the power for technology. Magic is used this way in Eberron. Eberron has airships, trains, and other examples of industrial technology, but these devices are powered by spells, magic items, and Elementals instead of nonmagical batteries and engines. Instantaneous, long-distance communication is possible in Eberron through Sending Stones staffed by members of a dragonmarked house; the result is akin to a magical telegraph network. Similarly, the residents of your society might have access to magic items that duplicate the effects of phones, automobiles, and similar modern conveniences.

#### Laws

Even a novice spellcaster, able to cast nothing more than a cantrip, can use their magic to kill. Powerful mages can secretly observe individuals from afar, probe the thoughts of others, or control minds. It stands to reason that magical societies would impose laws to restrict the use of invasive, coercive, and destructive magic.

When creating your magical world, consider how the law restricts the use of magic, if at all. Are mages punished only when their magic hurts others? Are certain spells always restricted, or does anything go?

##### Outlawed Magic

The simplest magic law is the strictest: a ban on all magic. Laws like this come about because a society has a long history of conflict with magical enemies, because magic is blamed for environmental or social ills, or because of simple superstition. Power-hungry mages who run a society might outlaw the use of magic by anyone other than the ruling class.

###### Vilhon Reach

Several regions of the Vilhon Reach in the Forgotten Realms curtail or ban magic, particularly magic used to charm or injure others.

##### Focusing on the Crime

Some magical societies consider laws against the use of magic unnecessary when existing laws already prohibit the harmful effects of magic. After all, murder is murder, whether the victim was killed with a sword, a spell, or the killer's bare hands.

For example, a large city like Greyhawk or Waterdeep might have laws against theft and laws that prohibit spying on a person in their home. An individual caught performing such acts would be guilty of a crime whether they cast the Invisibility spell or used nonmagical means.

Magic might count as an aggravating factor during sentencing, however. An individual found guilty of murder who killed the victim with the Finger of Death spell might be given a harsher sentence by a judge.

##### Frontier Justice

In remote regions that lack effective law enforcement, individuals and communities decide how magic and mages are treated. Residents might be suspicious of magic and intolerant of public spellcasting. Or they might become antagonistic toward mages who use magic in a way the residents don't like. As a third option, a single law enforcement official, such as a sheriff or marshal, might decide which mages are allowed to practice their craft undisturbed and which are driven out of town.

###### Ten-Towns

In the remote settlements of Ten-Towns in the Forgotten Realms, an innkeeper might hang up a "No Spells Allowed" sign and hire a bouncer or two to enforce this rule. Putting up such a sign is the innkeeper's right, but the town guard (if there is a town guard) is under no obligation to enforce it.

#### Commerce

How magic intertwines with commerce in your world is a key part of how your characters interact with it.

How easily can your characters find magic item shops or mages to purchase spellcasting services from? How accessible are magic items of higher rarity, such as Legendary magic items or Artifacts? Do mages' guilds regulate the accessibility of magic?

##### Magic Item Shops

*[Image: Members of House Deneith in Eberron work as paid, magic-wielding bodyguards. (book/AU/031-03-004.house-deneith.webp)]*

While Spell Scrolls and Potions of Healing are included in gear a character can buy in the Player's Handbook, the Dungeon Master's Guide leaves it up to the DM to decide what magic items are available to purchase in a campaign. A variety of magic item shops might be common in your world.

If you want Rare or rarer magic items to feel special, treat them like antiquities or fine art. A magic item shop in such a setting could be owned and operated by a single person who has deep knowledge of magical lore and contacts all over the world. When an adventurer asks for a specific magic item, the shop owner can procure it eventually, but the search takes time and leads to an adventure.

If magic items are more common in your world, a magic item shop in a city might look more like a pawnshop. All kinds of magic items would line the shelves, including duplicates of especially useful items such as Bags of Holding. The owner might be assisted by several individuals who specialize in security and deterring theft.

##### Magical Businesses

While any mage can sell their spellcasting services, your magical society might be filled with mages who use magic to earn a living in more interesting ways. The Player's Handbook presents availability and cost details for spellcasting services, but you can alter this availability as best suits your world.

For example, a novice spellcaster who can cast the Prestidigitation spell could operate a laundry service, quickly and easily cleaning clothes. A chef could use the same spell to flavor food for an hour. Cantrips such as Elementalism, Guidance, Mending, and Spare the Dying are also well suited for a humble but reliable magical career.

Magical businesses could take dramatic forms in your setting. Eberron is full of examples, from the magical artificers of House Cannith to the magical bodyguards of House Deneith.

##### Mages' Guilds

A mages' guild is an alliance of mages who run a robust magical business in a city. Such guilds are a staple of fantasy roleplaying games, especially campaigns with roots in D&D's earliest editions and settings, such as Greyhawk.

###### Guild Practices

A successful mages' guild works hand in hand with the local law enforcement. Typically, local laws don't prohibit spellcasting or making magic items, but they prohibit those who don't belong to the guild from making a profit by providing services that fall under the guild's exclusive concerns.

The local law enforcement typically includes members who are mages—and members of the guild. Both the guild and law enforcement tend to spend considerable time and effort monitoring mages in the city, enforcing guild rules and looking for mages avoiding guild membership.

###### Membership

Mages in a guild enjoy a monopoly on magical commerce. They expect to be paid an agreed-on amount of money for their services. Further, the guild is an excellent place for networking with other mages, and it probably operates a guildhall that includes a library of magical tomes and spellbooks as well as dining facilities, sleeping areas, and more. A guildhall could serve as an excellent base of operations for a group of characters connected to the guild.

Guild members are expected to pay dues and obey the guild's rules. In this way, a mages' guild in your world could serve as your characters' employer—as well as their debt holder.

###### Rank

Membership in the guild might be structured into three or more ranks. While members of the lowest rank have no influence in the guild and are expected to assist senior members for little or no pay, high-ranking members enjoy a wealthy lifestyle, authority in the guild, and the assistance of lower-ranking members.

###### Campaign Role

A mages' guild can serve as a group patron for characters or as an adversary that must be overcome. As a patron, the guild offers access to spellbooks, a comfortable refuge from danger, and magical allies knowledgeable on numerous subjects.

But a mages' guild might instead be an oppressive organization that ruthlessly exploits its monopoly and harasses nonmembers. Your campaign could focus on ousting the guild's leaders and replacing them with egalitarian leadership.

#### Magical Armies

*[Image: A War Wizard of Cormyr casts a powerful Evocation spell on the battlefield. (book/AU/032-03-005.war-wizard-of-cormyr.webp)]*

Fantasy realms raise and maintain armies to defend their territories. A society steeped in magic might establish magical armies to complement its regular troops. What benefits do magical armies provide, and what forms do they take?

##### War Wizards

A magical army might boast mages in its ranks, either mixed in among ordinary soldiers or forming elite units composed entirely of spellcasters. The War Wizards of Cormyr in the Forgotten Realms is an archetypal example of this kind of elite unit.

War wizards defy mage stereotypes. They often wear armor and wield martial weapons, resembling Eldritch Knights. Alternatively, a war wizard might wear simple robes and wield a staff but focus on damaging spells. These mages function as mobile artillery units, protected by martial soldiers. Spellguards (see x) might feature prominently in such an army.

##### Magical Minions

A magical army might supplement or even replace ordinary soldiers with magical minions. During the Last War, Eberron's armies extensively used magical minions, with the nation of Karrnath fielding armies of zombies and skeletons while Cyre and other nations used constructs. Outside Eberron, other magical realms fill their armies with conjured creatures or elementals summoned from the Inner Planes.

Fielding magical minions allows commanders and martial soldiers to remain safely behind the battle line while the minions participate in combat. A realm heavily dependent on magical minions might enter war more quickly than other realms and take more risks on the battlefield, brushing off the loss of magical minions as insignificant since they're easily replaced.

##### Magical Fortifications

A traditional castle isn't much of a defense against mages who can cast spells such as Fly, Passwall, and Teleport. Fortunately, a realm with magical resources has many magical ways to defend itself. Defensive measures such as the Wall of Force, Globe of Invulnerability, and Antimagic Field spells cover a small area for a limited time and are useful in emergencies. But options such as the Forbiddance and Guards and Wards spells last much longer and can provide layers of magical defenses. Entrances can be protected with the Glyph of Warding spell.

A fortification might be protected by magical defenders that negate an enemy's advantages. For example, flying assailants could be countered with flying defenders, such as soldiers mounted on griffins, hippogriffs, or pegasi.

#### Mastering Magic

*[Image: In your world, magical instruction might involve hundreds of students learning from masters in an arena such as this one. (book/AU/033-03-006.magical-arena.webp)]*

If magic suffuses your world, consider how its inhabitants learn their magical craft. Is magic laboriously taught by an elderly expert to a single apprentice? Do aspiring mages enroll at an academy where they're taught alongside others? Or do novice spellcasters learn another way?

The forms of magical instruction can be described in four ways: solitary study, apprenticeship, academic instruction, or cultural immersion, where magical instruction is readily available to all. Most magical societies rely on a single type of magical instruction. You might decide, however, that different kinds of instruction are prevalent in different parts of the world. For example, a magical realm might boast a magical academy with hundreds of students, but in the realm's capital, magic might be so common that everyone knows a spell or two thanks to the cultural prevalence of magic in everyday life.

##### Solitary Study

When magic is learned through solitary study, each mage understands magic slightly differently. For example, one mage might conceive of magic as influenced by the stars and planets, while another conceptualizes magic as an imperceptible force that connects all things. Both mages can cast spells of equivalent strength and effect, but their understanding of the underlying origin of magic varies.

###### Warlocks

In a society that relies on solitary study, every mage is self-taught. Warlocks, who cobble together magical power from pacts and little-understood scraps of lore, are the most common kind of spellcaster in these societies. Wizards, who traditionally rely on learning from others, might be rare.

##### Apprenticeship

When magic is learned through apprenticeship, magical instruction is limited to one-on-one relationships between an expert and a novice. An experienced mage might have many apprentices in a lifetime but teach only one at a time. As a result, there are always more candidates for apprenticeship than mentors, and competition among potential apprentices might be intense. Experienced mages might screen candidates or develop elaborate tests for prospective apprentices, measuring their intelligence, loyalty, magical acumen, or ethical code. These tests might serve as low-level adventures in your world.

###### Campaigns

In a campaign focused on apprenticeship, the mentor becomes unusually important. Craft interesting mentors your players will enjoy interacting with. The archetypal mentor is enigmatic and seemingly omnipotent, but they can also be whimsical, grouchy, or absent-minded.

In addition to creating a memorable mentor, consider when and how your characters' apprenticeships end. Is a level 1 Wizard finished with their apprenticeship? Or do they remain an apprentice until level 3 or later? How does the mentor decide when the student has learned enough?

###### Sorcerers

Sorcerers flourish in societies focused on apprenticeship. Many sorcerers inherit their powers from a magical ancestor, or their powers might be shared among members of their family. These family connections make it easier to secure a mentor, and the family might have well-established traditions around recognizing, controlling, and mastering magic.

##### Academic Instruction

In a long-established magical society, the myriad magical philosophies taught by individual mentors eventually coalesce around the most successful teachers. When these teachers pool their resources to form a magical academy, large numbers of students can benefit from academic instruction. Styles of magical academies are further detailed in "x" below.

###### Wizards

Wizards flourish in a magical society with academic instruction. Dutifully showing up to school every day, books in hand, Wizards learn new spells in a classroom environment. During off hours, they practice magic, pursue magical interests on their own, and get into the sort of trouble students always find.

##### Cultural Immersion

Magic in your society might be so well understood and so widespread that aspiring mages can learn basic magic from everyday life. In such a society, books of magical instruction might be found at any bookstore or library. Everyone might know a spell or two, and learning a new one might be as easy as sitting down for a few hours with a friend and exchanging techniques. When magic is widely understood and a part of everyday life, you don't need to create magical academies or mentor figures to explain why characters know basic magic.

###### Bards

Bards—whose magic takes the form of music, dance, and other popular art forms—flourish in a society where magic is popular. To Bards, learning new magic might be as simple as overhearing a catchy tune in a tavern and reworking it for their own purposes.

##### Magical Academies

The magical academies in the worlds of D&D make useful models for your world-building. Consider the following when creating your magical academy.

*[Image: Magical academies sometimes have their own sports, such as the game of Mage Tower at Strixhaven. (book/AU/034-03-007.magical-academies.webp)]*

###### Everything Under One Roof

The most common form of magical academy is a single place where several kinds of magic are taught. For example, Strixhaven, detailed in Strixhaven: A Curriculum of Chaos, is a magical academy where five different campuses teach opposing magic philosophies. Similarly, the Tower of High Sorcery in the Dragonlance setting, described in Dragonlance: Shadow of the Dragon Queen, teaches that setting's three primary orders of magic.

An academy of this sort is home to mages with different philosophies, goals, and methods. These individuals are united around the higher goal of teaching magic to the next generation of students. A student at one of these academies has access to a wide variety of spells, magical traditions, and instructors. Because students come from far away, they benefit from interacting with people from all over the world.

This kind of academy provides players with a lot of character creation options. Characters of any background, species, class, or subclass might come together to study magic and are sent on adventures by teachers with competing agendas. A magical academy is an example of an esoteric institution group patron, detailed later in this chapter.

###### Rival Academies

A realm with a large population of aspiring mages might have multiple magical academies that each specialize in a different form of magic.

An academy of this sort might focus on a single kind of magic: for instance, magic from the Conjuration school, or bladesinging, an ancient elven technique of magical combat. Rivalries between such academies take forms ranging from athletic competitions to pranks, clandestine raids, or even physical violence.

Because all students at one such academy study a single kind of magic, the academy is less likely to function as the home base for your campaign. Instead, the rival academies likely feature in the backstories of characters who wield magic, serving as the source of the characters' early magical training. However, you might create a campaign in which graduates of these rival academies are brought together by a menace that threatens all the academies or even the entire realm.

> **Magical Academies in the Real World**
> In our world, old traditions speak of schools where eager students went to learn magic. Transylvanian legend describes the Scholomance, a school for sorcery where the devil taught ten students at a time. At the end of their term of instruction, nine students were allowed to depart, but the devil claimed the tenth. Other examples of magical academies in folklore include the Cave of Salamanca in Spain and Domdaniel, a magical school first mentioned in a French continuation of The Arabian Nights.

### Magical Group Patrons

Whether your heroes wish to advance the agenda of a famous mage or seek access to a magical academy's ample resources, a group patron with significant magical prowess can create the cohesion that holds a campaign together and help your game feel infused with magic.

This section presents six types of magical group patrons around which you can create campaigns, as summarized on the Magical Patron Options table.

Each sample patron includes examples from the D&D multiverse and outlines for patronage campaigns. You can incorporate these into your game or use them as inspiration for your own magical group patron.

This section also explores the option of your group's patron hiding their identity for reasons benevolent or malign, which can be used with any of the group patrons that follow.

| --- | --- |
| x | A powerful, often famous mage with clear goals that span the multiverse |
| x | An organization dedicated to education, research, or containing dangerous magic |
| x | A non-Humanoid mage or magical creature with a mysterious, timeless outlook |

#### How Patrons Work

No matter their details, group patrons provide adventurers with some common benefits.

##### Group Assistance

Having a group patron gives an adventuring group a common purpose, which inspires better coordination in the form of guidance and encouragement. As a result of this connection, each member of your party can grant Advantage on a D20 Test made by another member of the party. To grant Advantage in this way, you and your target must be able to see or hear each other, and neither can have the Incapacitated condition. After granting Advantage in this way, you can't do so again until you finish a Long Rest.

##### Perks

Group patrons offer characters perks ranging from a steady wage to access to libraries and magical resources to unexpected allies. Specific perks are presented in the description of each group patron.

#### Option: Make Your Patron Mysterious

Concealing the true identity of your group patron can be a fun campaign linchpin, especially if your game involves magic-themed intrigue. Any of the magical group patrons described in this chapter might have reasons to stay anonymous. If you use a mysterious patron, create a strong reason the patron is hiding their identity.

##### High-Profile Patron

A group patron might remain anonymous because they're incredibly powerful, famous or infamous, or otherwise high profile, and their identity being known would undermine the characters' goals or ability to operate clandestinely. Or the patron might have reasons they wish to remain publicly unassociated with the characters. For example, perhaps the patron is a powerful political figure who can't publicly take sides in a civil dispute but wants the characters to side with freedom fighters over greedy merchants.

##### Ulterior Motives

*[Image: Powerful patrons are eager to sponsor young heroes, but their intent is sometimes unclear. (book/AU/035-03-008.powerful-patrons.webp)]*

On the other hand, a patron might hide their identity because their motives are malevolent. The patron might try to trick the characters into performing deeds that destabilize a fragile peace or empower an evil overlord. Or a patron might remain anonymous because the characters would never knowingly work for them, especially if the patron is nefarious and the heroes are upstanding.

> > — Alustriel

##### Concealing Their Identity

Mysterious magical patrons don't show their true faces to the characters, communicating via anonymous letters, prearranged signals, and networks of underlings similarly unaware of the patron's identity. A mysterious patron might refer to themself with a code name that fits the nature of the missions they ask the characters to undertake. In this case, the characters can't gain benefits associated with the patron's identity, such as the archmage's and esoteric institution's Documentation perks or the magical entity's Name Drop perk. Instead, the characters must find ways other than invoking their patron's name to achieve their goals.

###### Using Magic

Since magical patrons are typically powerful mages, they use high-level spell effects to ensure the characters don't discover their identity. A patron might repeatedly use the Programmed Illusion spell to convey information to the characters via fictional, illusory agents. If a patron must interact with a character, the patron might use the Seeming spell to significantly alter their appearance. Similarly, a patron might use the Mirage Arcane spell to conceal their lair's true location behind an illusory waterfall or trees. As your characters gain power, ensure their patron uses magic that the characters are unlikely to dispel.

###### Keeping the Characters on Board

If you use a mysterious group patron in your game, it's important for the characters to remain convinced that the patron's goals align with theirs. Attribute to the patron's code name deeds that are benevolent or helpful to the characters. Or ensure the patron compensates the characters well for their work, giving the characters a practical reason to continue working for them.

##### The Big Reveal

Eventually, the novelty of being sponsored by a mysterious group patron will wear thin. The characters might lose interest in working for someone unwilling to reveal their identity. They might begin to distrust or become frustrated with the patron.

When the time is right, you might reveal the patron's identity. You can do so suddenly, with the patron meeting the characters in person to thank them for their work, or more subtly.

###### Dropping Hints

As the characters complete more of the patron's missions, the patron might begin to drop—intentionally or unintentionally—coded hints about their identity. Investigating these clues can become a fun side quest for the characters.

###### Benevolent Patron

Once the characters have solved the clues, the patron might appear in person to congratulate them on discovering the secret. The patron might have intended to reveal themself once the characters proved themselves worthy through solving the clues, or the characters might have preempted the patron's plans. Either way, this gradual approach provides satisfaction to characters eager to learn their patron's identity.

###### Malevolent Patron

If the patron's motives are malicious, the characters might react with horror to their patron's identity. In this case, providing a satisfying conclusion to your campaign involves the characters confirming their patron's identity and formulating a plan for dealing with them. That might involve leaving the patron's employ abruptly, turning the tables on the patron to discredit them, confronting the patron in combat, or another option.

Follow your players' lead in how to wrap up the campaign. You might decide that the patron anticipated the characters' realization, but keep in mind that defeating this duplicitous patron allows a satisfying conclusion.

#### Archmage

As incredibly powerful spellcasters, archmages are capable of impressive deeds. Beyond slaying dragons or destroying undead, archmages have saved (and ruled!) entire cities, driven back armies, defeated the forces of the Nine Hells or the Abyss, and traveled in time. The mightiest archmages achieve magical mastery over the physical world. Some no longer age, spending centuries amassing unfathomable magical knowledge and prowess and creating spells they name after themselves.

With archmages' great learning and magical capabilities comes even greater willpower. Standing among the multiverse's strongest personalities, archmages personify stubbornness (even among the best) and self-centeredness (prominent among the worst). This unyielding nature translates into a voracious drive, whether it's to protect the weak, combat the wicked, amass the most knowledge, or take the biggest risks.

##### Archmage Motivations

Archmages might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **Emissaries Are Needed.** Emissaries Are Needed Archmages amass allies and commune with powerful leaders during their travels throughout the multiverse. An archmage hires the characters to travel to these allies and request their aid—or aid those allies—in the archmage's pursuit of friendly relations or magical research.
- **Evil Is Rising.** Evil Is Rising Benevolent archmages recognize the sheer power of unchecked evil and are sensitive to such forces amassing strength or plotting terrible deeds. An archmage who discovers complex and wicked schemes hires the characters to complete tasks that undermine those schemes.
- **Lieutenants Are Required.** Lieutenants Are Required An archmage who identifies numerous threats plaguing a certain area hires the characters to face a host of lesser threats so the archmage can focus on powerful villainous leaders.

- **Alustriel Silverhand.** Alustriel Silverhand One of the most powerful mages in the Forgotten Realms setting, Alustriel has been a proponent of good and enemy of evil for centuries. Alustriel is an immortal human daughter of Mystra, a god of magic, and ruled the city of Silverymoon for centuries. Alustriel maintains safe houses scattered across the planes for her allies as they promote compassion throughout the multiverse.
- **Vajra Safahr.** Vajra Safahr Chief mage of Waterdeep in the Forgotten Realms setting, the human Vajra is the youngest head in the history of Blackstaff Academy, a school for mages. While Vajra fends off threats to her city and her university, she also faces political conflicts from those who would unseat her from her place of prestige.

##### Archmage Patron Perks

Characters in a party whose patron is an archmage gain the following perks.

###### Compensation

Archmages amass considerable wealth during their adventures and travels, and archmage patrons generously compensate their underlings. The nature of your work for an archmage influences how you're paid. On average, the archmage pays each member of your group 2 GP per day (enough to sustain a Comfortable (2 GP per Day) lifestyle). Or you might be paid (at least 300 GP) each time you complete a task the archmage assigns.

###### Documentation

Each member of your group has identification noting their affiliation with the archmage, which carries clout in areas where the archmage is known. If the archmage is the ruler of a city or nation, the documentation grants the characters appropriate authority in that realm.

###### Magical Resources

Archmages collect untold magic items and magical curiosities, though these collections are often scattered among far-flung sanctums. An archmage can give you any Common or Uncommon magic item in the Dungeon Master's Guide, as long as possession of the item would help your group's tasks. When your group reaches level 11, the archmage can retrieve one Rare and one Very Rare magic item in between the group's tasks and award it to you as additional compensation. When your group reaches level 17, the archmage can retrieve one Rare, Very Rare, or Legendary magic item in between tasks and award it to you as additional compensation.

###### Spellcasting

Archmages can use their spellpower to help your missions. An archmage can cast any level 1 or lower spell for you at no cost, as long as the spell's effects would aid your work. The archmage can cast any level 2–8 spell for you at half the usual cost.

#### Esoteric Institution

An esoteric institution might be a magical academy, an order of monks researching lost magical techniques, or any other organization focused on the study, preservation, and use of magical lore. Institutions usually maintain a base of operations such as a campus, monastery, or remote fortress. The institution might have a large staff including administrators, teachers, groundskeepers, and guards. In addition to an excellent library, it probably has a secure vault for storing dangerous magic items, and more than one Artifact might be housed there.

Esoteric institutions have a long history that stretches back generations. In that time, an institution has made enemies and collected magic items that have been stored away and forgotten. The institution's headquarters might have secret chambers protected by puzzles and traps. Despite its reclusive or insular nature, the institution monitors the world beyond its walls. When it finds magic being used for evil or learns of long-forgotten lore to be recovered, it sends adventurers on missions.

*[Image: The Tower of High Sorcery in the world of Dragonlance charges heroes with protecting magical secrets from a destructive draconic army. (book/AU/038-03-011.tower-of-high-sorcery.webp)]*

##### Esoteric Institution Motivations

An esoteric institution might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **The Characters Are Students.** The Characters Are Students The characters are students at an academy such as the Tower of High Sorcery in the Dragonlance setting or Strixhaven University and are sent on missions as part of their studies. The institution doesn't intentionally send the characters on dangerous missions, and it sends rescue teams if the characters get into trouble while pursuing goals tied to the academy.
- **The Institution Needs Field Agents.** The Institution Needs Field Agents An esoteric institution includes sages, scholars, and researchers, but few warriors and explorers. The institution wants to hire the characters for missions deemed too dangerous for its own staff, such as rescuing students in peril.
- **The Characters Are Specialists.** The Characters Are Specialists An esoteric institution seeks out the characters because the characters are experts in a particular field relevant to a current problem or interest. For example, if the characters recently defeated a powerful vampire, the institution hires them to recover that vampire's hidden library of magical tomes.

The following organizations from the D&D multiverse could serve as esoteric institution group patrons:

Its members maintain several hidden and well-protected monasteries, but the largest is Watchers' Stronghold in the Mistlands, a piece of the disintegrating domain of Darkon. The order is stretched thin, outnumbered and outmatched by the horrors found in the Domains of Dread, and it's quick to hire adventurers willing to venture into dangerous sites to recover powerful items or spells so they can't be used for evil. More information on the Order of the Guardians and Darkon can be found in Ravenloft: The Horrors Within.

This god maintains a great library at the city's center. The library enjoys a reputation as a matchless repository of knowledge. The ibis-headed god employs the characters as agents who travel through the city of Sigil, City of Doors and the planes to find books and bring them back to the library for copying and collection. - **Tower of High Sorcery.** Tower of High Sorcery In the Dragonlance setting, there were once five Towers of High Sorcery, where mages were trained. Due to disaster and tragedy, only one remains. Characters in a party whose patron is an esoteric institution gain the following perks.

The institution pays for the work you do on its behalf. The nature of your employment influences your compensation. On average, the institution pays each member of your group 1 GP per day (or enough to sustain a Modest (1 GP per Day) lifestyle). Or you might be paid a bounty (at least 250 GP) for each magic item or piece of lore you bring back from your adventures and give to the institution.

###### Documentation

Each member of your group has identification noting their affiliation with the institution, which carries clout in areas where the institution is known. For magical academies such as the Tower of High Sorcery or Strixhaven, this is a letter on official stationery marked with the academy's seal, but for secretive organizations like the Order of the Guardians, the documentation takes the form of a signet ring or brooch marked with a discreet symbol.

Esoteric institutions include expansive libraries specializing in magical knowledge. Once you reach level 13, you instead can issue the Research order to the institution as if it were an Archive.

You have access to the institution's library of Spell Scrolls and spellbooks, as well as supplies for copying spells. - **Tool.** Tool You gain proficiency with a tool of your choice.

#### Magical Entity

A magical entity is a powerful creature of mysterious or otherworldly origin. The entity might openly rule the realm in which they live, or they might be a mysterious figure that manipulates events from behind the scenes. Regardless, the magical entity is old, extremely knowledgeable, and frightening.

The magical entity is surrounded by legends and superstition. Villagers and townsfolk in the region know the entity by name. Most magical entities are feared as monsters, even if they're benevolent, though some are revered as honorable creatures that protect the realm.

##### Magical Entity Motivations

Magical entities might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **Bound to a Place.** Bound to a Place The magical entity is bound to a particular location and can't leave. While supremely powerful and all but omniscient in their own realm, the being needs the characters to accomplish objectives beyond the entity's reach.
- **Like Recognizes Like.** Like Recognizes Like The magical entity sees something in the characters that reminds the entity of themself. A good patron likely admires heroes who are kind and just, while an evil patron might be impressed by characters they perceive as proud and cruel.
- **Too Famous to Travel.** Too Famous to Travel The magical entity is so well known—and feared—that they can't travel without attracting unwanted attention and alerting their enemies. The entity needs agents who can avoid notice.

##### Magical Entity Patrons

*[Image: Bahamut, a patron of good dragons, enlists heroes to fight against terrible evils. (book/AU/039-03-012.bahamut.webp)]*

The following beings from the D&D multiverse could serve as magical entity patrons:

- **Baba Yaga.** Baba Yaga An arch-hag known as the Mother of All Witches, Baba Yaga travels the multiverse in her chicken-legged hut, pursuing inscrutable goals. She's also the adoptive mother of the famous witch Tasha. Baba Yaga might hire characters of all alignments to seek esoteric magical knowledge or to act as her emissaries in formal political environments. Her goals and motivations are mysterious, but she offers great power to those she favors.
- **Bahamut.** Bahamut Bahamut is a god of good in numerous settings and is like a king to metallic dragons. He appears to mortals in various guises, often accompanied by seven yellow canaries that are gold dragons in disguise. In the world of Dragonlance, he has appeared as Fizban the Fabulous, a bumbling mage whose dramatic "accidents" often have hidden purposes. Bahamut is an excellent patron for good-aligned groups; he's unlikely to work with evil characters unless he thinks they can be convinced to change their ways. He's likely to conceal his true nature from the characters.
- **Strahd von Zarovich.** Strahd von Zarovich Trapped in the domain of Barovia, the vampire Strahd von Zarovich is described in Ravenloft: The Horrors Within and Curse of Strahd. Strahd wields supreme power in his homeland, but he can't leave it. He needs the characters to be his eyes and ears outside Barovia. Supremely egotistical, Strahd finds the idea of concealing his true identity entertaining for a while, but once the party has done a task or two for him, the mask comes off and the vampire reveals himself. Knowingly working with Strahd for an extended time likely won't appeal to most heroic parties, though Strahd could make a great ongoing patron for amoral adventurers.

Your patron might reward you with Common or Uncommon magic items, especially consumables such as potions and Spell Scrolls.

##### A Magical Entity Campaign

A campaign driven by a magical entity patron might unfold according to this outline:

- **Levels 1–4.** Levels 1–4 The campaign begins as the characters stumble into the entity's domain and prove themselves against creatures the entity considers enemies. Aberrations such as Grells, Gricks, and Grimlocks are all thematically appropriate foes.
- **Levels 5–10.** Levels 5–10 The magical entity approaches the characters or invites them to the entity's lair and offers to become their group patron, sending them on missions against monstrous foes. Githyanki and slaadi make good antagonists for this tier of play.
- **Levels 11–16.** Levels 11–16 The mysterious mastermind controlling these Aberrations—whom the characters haven't yet identified—captures or imprisons the magical entity, perhaps through the use of an Artifact, and the characters must rescue them. The characters battle Aboleths, Beholders, Death Tyrants, and Mind Flayer Arcanists.
- **Levels 17–20.** Levels 17–20 The mysterious mastermind is revealed as a creature similar to the group patron, but different in nature. For example, Bahamut is captured by a group of Ancient Red Dragons. Or Strahd is at the mercy of a powerful Lich, such as Acererak or Vecna. These two liches are further described in Vecna: Eve of Ruin, including game statistics for Vecna.

#### Political Faction

A political faction group patron is tied to the governing structure of a magical realm. A political faction usually has great wealth and access to the realm's resources, but the faction leaders are politicians and bureaucrats, not adventurers. Even if the leaders are powerful mages, they have no interest in risking their own lives. Instead, they hire the characters as spies or agents.

Campaigns with a political faction group patron combine high magic with political intrigue. The characters might be kept in the dark by their patron faction, which gives them only the information they absolutely need (and sometimes not even that). The faction sends the party into dangerous situations that force them to improvise.

##### Political Faction Motivations

A political faction might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **The Characters Are Residents.** The Characters Are Residents If the characters live in the realm where the faction governs, the faction believes the characters are easier to recruit and more likely to be loyal. The faction might exert legal pressure on the characters, forcing them to do as the faction requires.
- **The Characters Are Foreign.** The Characters Are Foreign The faction prefers to hire outsiders who can operate in neighboring realms without attracting attention, and who know the languages and customs of the faction's rivals.
- **The Characters Are Deniable Assets.** The Characters Are Deniable Assets The political faction hires the adventures without official sanction. The party is sent on dangerous missions in far-off lands, and if they're captured, the political faction denies any connection or responsibility for the party's actions.

#### Trickster

Tricksters are powerful beings—often archfey—who are known for their whimsical nature. A trickster can take many forms and might appear pleasant, angry, or simply otherworldly. But at their core, the trickster is unpredictable, their priorities and goals changing with the seasons or even from day to day.

Tricksters make good patrons for groups that prefer social interaction, exploration, and nonviolent conflict resolution over combat. Many tricksters prefer humiliating and pranking their enemies to killing them. Groups with a trickster patron are likely to spend time in the Feywild, visiting Domains of Delight and learning the distinctive rules of each.

##### Trickster Motivations

A trickster might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **The Characters Are Interesting.** The Characters Are Interesting Tricksters are like magpies, driven to collect shiny baubles. Adventurers are colorful, larger-than-life personalities who are likely to attract a trickster's attention.
- **The Trickster Needs Plausible Deniability.** The Trickster Needs Plausible Deniability A trickster makes enemies, and those enemies often instantly recognize the trickster and are alert to disguises. The trickster needs agents who aren't known to be associated with the trickster.
- **The Characters Are Expendable Assets.** The Characters Are Expendable Assets The trickster needs dangerous tasks accomplished and is unwilling to send their more experienced agents out of concern those agents might not return. The characters' deaths won't inconvenience the trickster.

#### Wizards' Circle

Powerful spellcasters occasionally form an alliance known as a wizards' circle to pursue goals beyond the reach of any individual. Wizards' circles are often temporary, formed to resolve a specific problem that threatens the world or the multiverse. But even archmages need friends or allies, and the members of a wizards' circle might make their alliance permanent.

In many ways, a wizards' circle is like an archmage group patron, with the added complication that the circle consists of several strong-willed individuals who don't necessarily like each other. The characters get caught in the middle of long debates and old rivalries between members of the circle, and it can be hard to satisfy one member of the circle without alienating another.

##### Wizards' Circle Motivations

A wizards' circle might patronize a group for the following reasons:

- **The Characters Balance Each Other.** The Characters Balance Each Other Each member of the wizards' circle has their own preferences when selecting agents and operatives. The party represents a collection of these preferences, with each member of the circle backing one member of the party. Although each character is backed by a single member, the circle as a whole serves as a group patron for the characters.
- **The Characters Have Special Skills.** The Characters Have Special Skills A wizards' circle lacks expertise in fields outside members' specialties, including types of magic outside their bailiwick, martial prowess, or exploration skills. The characters provide useful abilities that the circle members lack.
- **Pawns on the Chessboard.** Pawns on the Chessboard The wizards' circle supports the group because the characters feature in the circle's long-term plans. The circle members might know one of the characters is unknowingly a noble and thus a potential candidate for the throne, or they might have identified one of the characters as the only person who can safely wield a dangerous Artifact. These facts might not be known to the characters.

## Chapter 4: Magical Factions

*[Image: Mages engage in a magical duel while members of the Crucible Keepers look on. (book/AU/043-04-001.ch-4-splash.webp)]*

Many scholars and practitioners of magic seek ever-greater prowess and status, but few can achieve these individually. Instead, those pursuing mystical knowledge often join influential groups. Numerous in membership and great in achievement, these factions accept adventurers into their folds, offering resources and knowledge in exchange for loyalty and service.

Each faction described in this chapter has its own outlook and goals, such as providing magical advice and entertainment to influence the powerful, researching alien forces from beyond the stars, and uncovering the secrets of life and undeath.

Faction members exchange magical discoveries, mentor pupils, and learn from more experienced colleagues. Members are united by shared specialties and philosophies. Although these organizations' disparate objectives often bring them into conflict with each other, members of different factions might also unite over common goals.

You can use these factions as patrons, as villains, or as organizations the characters can join. Adapt them to your world or campaign as needed.

### Faction Descriptions

This chapter includes details on the following factions:

- x The discreet enchanters of the Bejeweled Conclave make themselves invaluable to powerful clients as elite entertainers, advisers, and mediators while amassing clients' secrets.
- x Reclusive transmuters obsessed with mutations, the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn seek guidance from alien intelligences.
- x The necromancers of the Covenant of the Grave study death and undeath with scientific precision, seeking esoteric knowledge through rigorous research.
- x From volcanic eruptions to shore-shredding hurricanes, no natural disaster is too dangerous for the daring Crucible Keepers to study in their pursuit of ever-escalating applications for Evocation magic.
- x The Horizon Weavers' conjurers practice summoning and teleportation as they explore and chart the multiverse.
- x The spies and saboteurs of the Ninth Quill steal lore and artifacts from other powerful mages; many members do so to safeguard these treasures, but others are motivated by greed. The Ninth Quill also stokes dissent among the other factions to keep them from growing too powerful.
- x The showy illusionists of the Phantasmic Circus entertain eager audiences with impossible spectacles.
- x The diligent oracles of the Seers of Sea and Sky divine truths in the movements of the wind and waves.
- x The abjurers of the Sheltering Hands uplift the downtrodden and protect those in need.

### Bejeweled Conclave

The Bejeweled Conclave is known as an organization of professional entertainers and mediators who cater to wealthy clients. They host glamorous parties, serve as courtiers, broker deals, and provide entertainment for exclusive functions.

But the Bejeweled Conclave wields far more influence than its publicly known services indicate. Consummate agents behind the throne, the enchanters of the Bejeweled Conclave cultivate elite connections and shift the balance of power in cities across the world.

#### Goals and Activities

*[Image: This unassuming baker is, in reality, a member of the Bejeweled Conclave's Whisper Network. (book/AU/045-04-003.bejeweled-conclave-baker.webp)]*

The Bejeweled Conclave seeks to increase its sway across the multiverse. The faction engages skilled diplomats, courtiers, and entertainers and hones their abilities. Enchanters and spies receive a more clandestine, but no less thorough, education. The conclave prizes subtlety and secrecy above all; members are taught that it's better to fail a mission and keep the conclave's secrets than to succeed at the cost of exposing the faction's true goals.

The Bejeweled Conclave operates in major cities throughout the multiverse. Distance keeps members from knowing all their peers, but the faction is quick to celebrate successes, building loyalty and camaraderie within its ranks. While all members learn conversational tricks to offer convincing praise, the compliments members bestow on each other are usually genuine.

Conclave members understand that overtly using Enchantment magic risks exposing them as more than entertainers and courtiers. If a member must modify a noble's memories or magically dominate a general, they undertake significant planning to conceal their involvement.

#### Leadership and Organization

More experienced members of the Bejeweled Conclave oversee operations assigned to junior members. Valuable secrets are reported through this hierarchy up to the faction's highest ranks, referred to as the Whisper Network. These leaders hoard a treasure trove of secrets; there are few facts about the movers and shakers of civilization that the Bejeweled Conclave doesn't know.

Conclave members are trusted to act alone when necessary, such as when an important client insists on hiring only a single mediator or companion. When possible, though, senior members politely request that their "junior clerks" or "associates in training" handle more workaday aspects of their affairs. Such requests, which might include subtle magical compulsion, are a ruse to bring another trained member to operate from the shadows. As a result, clients who planned to planned to hire only one member of the Bejeweled Conclave often find themselves engaging two or three.

##### Whisper Network

Members of the Whisper Network keep their identities private, even from their peers. The only exception is Madame Hestia Ilverne (Medium Noble Prodigy), a courteous human Whisper Network member who heads the conclave's public-facing efforts. Rumors within the faction claim that Madame Ilverne is among the lowest ranked of the leaders, but she seems nothing but pleased with her position.

Whether members of the Whisper Network work as music instructors, clerks, dancers, or cooks, they keep an eye on all aspects of Bejeweled Conclave activities. If they have edicts or missions to impart, they do so through Madame Ilverne. When she casually mentions a task to a member with the words "The Network would prefer a favor," all comprehend the seriousness of the request.

##### Lurewood Mansion

A regal manor house in the garden district of a major city, Lurewood Mansion is the conclave's academy. The previous owners donated their property generations ago for unknown reasons. The manor's architecture has been adapted to include numerous salons and studies, as well as a fine kitchen.

In the studies, members of the Bejeweled Conclave learn to negotiate deals and defuse arguments. In the salons, singers, dancers, and musicians practice entertainment suitable for elevated private parties, from subdued chamber music to sensual dances to heart-wrenching ballads. New faction members take lessons here no matter how polished they are when recruited, if for no other reason than to bolster the illusion that negotiation and entertainment are the Bejeweled Conclave's prime objectives.

Beneath the mansion, a dizzying maze of chambers connected through locked doors and one-way mirrors facilitates practice in eavesdropping, lock-picking, quick changes, and stealthy escapes. Other rooms allow members to rest out of sight. The heart of the maze contains the mansion's best-kept secret: workrooms where enchanters teach apprentice mages the arts of twisting minds, magically hiding secrets, and erasing memories.

##### Joining the Conclave

Aspiring faction members can't join the Bejeweled Conclave by requesting admission; members inevitably deny that there are any openings in what they claim is simply a loose professional organization.

Entry is only by invitation. Senior members carefully watch entertainers and mediators who have impressive public reputations. Those who seem particularly shrewd and ambitious might receive an invitation for free training in their specialties at Lurewood Mansion, hosted by Madame Ilverne.

Once an invitee arrives, Madame Ilverne engages them in cryptic conversation. If the potential member impresses her with their poise, acumen, and discretion, she invites them to join the faction. Otherwise, she thanks them for their time and sends them on their way without ever indicating that the mansion is anything other than a professional academy.

### Bringers of Cosmic Dawn

*[Image: A member of the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn transforms a colleague into a strange creature. (book/AU/046-04-004.bringers-of-cosmic-dawn.webp)]*

A secretive organization of amoral transmuters, the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn receives abstruse directives from alien entities that hail from the dark spaces between stars. Faction members magically mutate plants and creatures in preparation for visitation by these beings—an event they believe is inevitable. They believe these entities will witness their glorious transmutations, be impressed, and bring about an exalted future full of mutated aberrations that serve transmuters' every whim.

#### Goals and Activities

The exact goals of the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn are inscrutable to many members, though all believe they are helping to usher an ascended age of existence. The faction draws unprincipled transmuters, fringe scientists, and irrational astronomers to its academy and observatory, the Cosmic Oculus, where they receive fractured instructions from the faction's five leaders, known as the Starspeakers.

These communications have a consistent message: Faction members must transmute the flora and fauna surrounding the Cosmic Oculus. Invariably, this involves transforming plants into hostile, flesh-eating specimens and mutating beasts to rearrange organs, add joints to limbs, and cause them to secrete slime. In doing so, the faction hopes to gain the attention of alien entities they believe will descend and warp all worlds in their path.

The faction members are united in purpose but not in execution. Some pursue their own theories of mutation and transfiguration, experimenting on themselves as much as on other subjects. Some instructions communicated by the Starspeakers conflict with others, as though the alien entities they seek to communicate with weren't working in concert. These contradictions mean that the faction's work advances erratically, with rejected experiments as common as transformations deemed to be in concordance with the aliens' will.

##### Dawnrise Canyon

Bringers of Cosmic Dawn members carry out most transmutations far from public scrutiny. The faction is based in the secluded Dawnrise Canyon, far below the clifftop where the Cosmic Oculus stands. The once-tranquil canyon is now a dangerous valley of frightening creatures, hostile plants, aggressive transmuters, and wandering aberrations drawn to the unnatural landscape. Time moves erratically in the region thanks to frequent use of time-altering magic to speed up or slow down specific mutations.

##### Opposition to the Far Realm

While outsiders might not comprehend the distinction between the alien intelligences the Starspeakers commune with and the monsters of the Far Realm, Bringers of Cosmic Dawn members fear and reject the Far Realm as unknowable, indescribable, and inimical to life. The faction works to counter Far Realm incursions and expels any members who deal directly with that domain of incomprehensible horror.

#### Leadership and Organization

The Bringers of the Cosmic Dawn lacks a rigid hierarchy, but its leaders maintain careful control over the information they receive from both members' experiments and alien intelligences.

##### Member Roles

Certain members of the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn have special roles in the faction.

###### Starspeakers

The five enigmatic Starspeakers command the entire faction. None answer to any name besides their title, and they rarely leave the Cosmic Oculus except to measure progress within Dawnrise Canyon. Four Starspeakers (Medium Aberrant Cultists) are human. The fifth and most senior Starspeaker appears to be an elderly human astronomer, but she is actually a magically disguised Aboleth who has eschewed the Far Realm.

###### Worldwarpers

Most faction members work at the Cosmic Oculus or Dawnrise Canyon, but others, called Worldwarpers, venture into civilized lands to engage in more far-flung experimentation. Worldwarpers seek out willing recipients to, as they put it, "gain blessings from beyond the stars."

Worldwarpers might offer to improve a banker's garden with voracious plants to deter thieves, provide an aberration to help guard a prison, or augment a diplomat's body to produce intoxicating pheromones. Like the work in Dawnrise Canyon, these puzzling changes ostensibly further a wider agenda, but Worldwarpers are rarely aware of the end goals; they merely put pieces in place as commanded and report back to the Starspeakers.

###### Blots

The Starspeakers secretly assign faction members to find and eradicate Far Realm sympathizers. These individuals, known within the faction as Blots, also venture into places where the Far Realm's stain bleeds into the world, seeking to quash the Far Realm's presence wherever they find it.

Transmuters might seek out the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn even if they don't wish to advance the faction's goals. Although some transmuters escape the faction, far too many begin to believe the Starspeakers' ramblings and become devotees—or worse, they become unwilling subjects for experimental mutations when their disloyalty is uncovered.

##### Cosmic Oculus

Once an abandoned observatory, the Cosmic Oculus became the Bringers of Cosmic Dawn's base when the faction discovered that the facility seems to magically amplify the messages of entities between the stars. In addition to the Starspeakers, faction astronomers also live in the Cosmic Oculus to record psychic signals from deep space and make nightly measurements of celestial bodies.

Most faction members never see the inside of the Cosmic Oculus. The ramshackle buildings clustered along the cliff face between the observatory and the canyon have expanded and mutated on their own, and members have used spells such as Stone Shape and Fabricate to haphazardly extend walls, enclose laboratories, and create passageways through the rock.

### Covenant of the Grave

The Covenant of the Grave is a loose affiliation of necromancers and those interested in studying death and undeath through a disciplined, scientific lens. The covenant has no room for megalomaniacs who seek domination via hordes of undead minions.

These realists are aware that their studies, no matter how clinical, unnerve most people, so their members are unobtrusive and their laboratories hidden. The greatest covenant academy is the Pale Tower, a morbid, bone-white structure far from civilization.

#### Goals and Activities

Covenant members are typically more interested in deepening their knowledge than their power. They freely share their research with each other via correspondence, personal visits, or clandestine lectures, but they rarely reveal discoveries to those outside the organization. Many worry that their research might be misused for sinister, nonacademic ends. Others simply don't expect anyone but like-minded scholars to comprehend their work.

Unlike necromancers who incite undead uprisings, the Covenant of the Grave usually works to stymie undead hordes and return less powerful undead to the safer confines of the laboratory or grave. Members consider undead rampages dreadfully unscientific, if not outright embarrassing. They're keenly aware of the harm that such villainous necromancy brings to the faction's reputation. Their opposition to necromantic wickedness sometimes puts the faction in conflict with powerful undead such as liches, death knights, or vampires.

#### Leadership and Organization

Although an established hierarchy exists within the Covenant of the Grave, members are usually too engrossed in their own research to worry about rank.

##### Necrodemics and Gravetenders

Any faction member who shares a significant scholarly truth about death, decay, or undeath with the faction might gain the title of Necrodemic. Some members hold expanded titles such as Arisen Necrodemic or Necrodemic Embalmist, awarded for sharing more complex knowledge.

The title of Gravetender is held by the three head administrators of the Pale Tower. Emeritus administrators keep this title after retirement, so four to six Gravetenders usually exist at a time. Gravetenders set the overall direction for the faction but are usually bookish and circumspect, so change within the faction is gradual.

##### Joining the Covenant

*[Image: Members of the Covenant of the Grave show respect to undead individuals who allow themselves to be studied. (book/AU/047-04-005.covenant-of-the-grave.webp)]*

Any spellcaster with an interest in Necromancy magic or macabre research is likely to hear about the Covenant of the Grave, but the faction doesn't actively recruit. The covenant expects aspirants to independently identify its members and present a well-reasoned proposal for membership. A member who receives a qualified aspirant usually confers with other members before either taking on the aspirant as a research assistant or sending them to study at the Pale Tower.

##### Undead Members

Only a few members of the Covenant of the Grave are undead. Covenant members have little use for less powerful undead such as skeletons and zombies, and other undead are usually too consumed by their hungers to maintain the intellectual rigor the covenant expects. ##### The Pale Tower

The greatest concentration of covenant members resides in the Pale Tower. Rising from a noxious swamp, this bone-white structure is thousands of years old. The tower's small, dingy exterior belies its underground warren of classrooms, laboratories, and ossuaries.

In addition to being an academy and research institution, the Pale Tower is a repository of sinister necromantic magic that the covenant doesn't want misused. Rumors hold that malicious artifacts, lost Necromancy spells, and soul-staining magic items are locked away in vaults only the Gravetenders can access.

### Crucible Keepers

The Crucible Keepers consists of daredevil evokers who venture into natural or magical disasters to study the phenomenal power at play. Some members use their Evocation magic to counter disasters and help others, while other members revel in nature's destructive forces and take inspiration from them to enhance their own devastating spellcraft.

#### Goals and Activities

*[Image: Crucible Keepers love a good explosion. (book/AU/049-04-007.crucible-keepers.webp)]*

Crucible Keepers study Evocation magic to command energy and control powerful forces. They eschew solitary scholarship, preferring informal gatherings where they exchange ideas and try to impress their peers with spectacular displays of magical might.

Faction members might be thrill seekers, but they aren't fools. They plan their dangerous endeavors well in advance, spending days or weeks charting natural or magical phenomena before enjoying the rush of soaring through the eye of a hurricane or surfing a lava flow. They employ protective magic while engaging in these endeavors, and they're quick to chide fools who confront danger unprepared.

> > Overuse of powerful evocation magic can warp the land as well as the souls of those who become drunk on magical power. The Crucible Keepers' attempt to right these wrongs is noble.
> > — Alustriel

#### Leadership and Organization

Those drawn to the Crucible Keepers aren't the sort who like following orders. Ranks within the Crucible Keepers are unofficial, fluid, and based on frequent competitive challenges between members, with losers acknowledging winners' higher rank. Challenges usually involve magical duels, elaborate races, or dangerous stunts.

##### Joining the Crucible Keepers

The Crucible Keepers enthusiastically welcomes anyone who wants to try out for its ranks. Gaining membership is as simple as impressing any faction member with a clever display of Evocation magic, a wild physical stunt, or a dramatic parlor trick.

##### Eljar Charscale

The current head of the Crucible Keepers is a dragonborn named Eljar Charscale (Medium Evoker Archmage; see x). Originally a human, Eljar was executed by cruel generals when he refused to use his magic in an unpopular war. After his allies reincarnated him, Eljar fled in his new dragonborn body. He took the name Charscale on joining the Crucible Keepers.

Eljar loves the thrill of Evocation magic and is undefeated in duels of spellcraft. In addition to his magical talent, Eljar is respected for listening to everyone and offering sensible guidance. He began leading the Crucible Keepers only a few months ago after the former leader died in a forest fire.

Eljar has a strong antiwar stance that he disseminates throughout the organization. He has forbidden members of the Crucible Keepers from serving as military battle mages. Some evokers believe this stance restricts their career paths, but most understand the reasoning behind Eljar's policy.

### Horizon Weavers

The Horizon Weavers is a collection of far-flung conjurers, explorers, and scholars who practice summoning, teleportation, and planar travel.

Members of the Horizon Weavers have unparalleled wanderlust; they've seen sights and lived through experiences most would consider too wild to be true. However, the faction's planar sojourns have not gone unnoticed, and members now work to avert the attention of a malevolent extraplanar entity fixated on their organization.

#### Goals and Activities

Horizon Weavers hone their expertise in all forms of Conjuration magic, from summoning to creation to teleportation, with a focus on extraplanar travel. Powerful Horizon Weavers create gates between planes, while junior members catalog known gates or simply wander through to see what's on the other side. New faction members usually begin by traveling their own world to experience different climates and cultures before exploring other planes.

Many faction members are also amateur or professional artisans; some weave textiles as well as magic. Horizon Weavers often incorporate spells such as Find Familiar or Mage Hand into their everyday lives, using magic for everything from companionship to household chores.

##### Horizon Weaver Cloaks

Each new member who joins the faction receives a durable cloak with their initials sewn into the collar. Horizon Weavers recognize these garments on sight and seek to retrieve any that are lost or stolen.

A Horizon Weaver's cloak repels water and protects its wearer from most types of weather. The faction replaces damaged cloaks when necessary. Some members weave the threads of former cloaks into new ones.

Many Horizon Weavers incorporate their cloaks into magic items they create. The most popular choices are Cape of the Mountebank, Cloak of Protection, and Clothes of Mending.

##### The Lurker

A massive beast from the liminal space between planes menaces the Horizon Weavers. This entity, known as the Lurker, towers higher than mountains, with immense pincers and a single, enormous eye that radiates malice and reflects strange lights and lines.

Several months ago, when faction members first glimpsed the Lurker, a powerful team of Horizon Weavers set out to confront the monster. The team went missing, and the Lurker has since become more aggressive, snapping at some Horizon Weavers in the split second it takes them to magically travel between planes. Sometimes this shears the unlucky traveler in half; other times, the individual never reappears. These incidents are rare, but all Horizon Weavers worry about a potential Lurker strike with each planar trip.

> > Those who provide shelter and aid to explorers of this vast multiverse are friends to both me and Mystra.
> > — Alustriel

#### Leadership and Organization

As befits members of a society of explorers, Horizon Weavers are widely scattered. A wandering Horizon Weaver might go weeks or months without seeing another member, and a meeting might be coincidental. Senior faction members, called Gatemakers, settle disputes or provide advice, although reaching a Gatemaker often requires a cross-planar trek.

### Ninth Quill

*[Image: Members of the Ninth Quill plot the heist of magical lore. (book/AU/053-04-011.ninth-quill-plotting.webp)]*

The Ninth Quill is a faction of thieves, saboteurs, and spies who use magic to obtain and sequester powerful artifacts and knowledge by any means necessary—including robbing and sabotaging other factions. The faction's leaders consider their organization to be superior to factions focused on a specific school of magic and the safest custodian of dangerous lore and relics, but some members also steal valuables and undermine other organizations for personal gain.

#### Goals and Activities

Ninth Quill members' adaptability and diverse areas of expertise help them infiltrate other groups, often posing as new recruits or long-absent members from other lands.

The Ninth Quill has a few popular catchphrases that members use as meaningful mantras—or excuses for criminality. These phrases are described in the sections that follow.

##### Our Hands Are Safest

Ninth Quill members often steal magic items and research to keep them out of others' hands. They receive extensive training in handling dangerous artifacts and resisting the influence of sentient objects. In principle, these items are sequestered in the Ninth Quill vaults (which members insist are more secure than any other faction's), but less scrupulous members might keep certain items for their own use.

##### Others Learn So We Can Earn

When the Ninth Quill steals discoveries and relics that aren't particularly harmful, it sells them to wealthy patrons to fund its operations. Desperate buyers rebuffed by other factions, such as a necromancer rejected by the x or a transmuter suspicious of the x, might seek the services of the Ninth Quill to obtain such objects.

##### Discontent Is Our Best Defense

The Ninth Quill seeks to destabilize other factions that become too powerful. This often involves agents infiltrating a faction, then undertaking whisper campaigns, sabotaging experiments, and encouraging critical personnel to depart. The Ninth Quill agents quietly slip away once they have stoked sufficient inefficiency, paranoia, and infighting.

#### Leadership and Organization

The Ninth Quill values intelligence, resourcefulness, and loyalty. A significant majority of Ninth Quill agents use spellcasting to enhance their spy craft.

##### A Careful Hierarchy

Although Ninth Quill leaders take pains to hide their identities, the faction operates under a strict hierarchal system. Agents are the lowest ranking, followed by Lieutenants, followed by Masters. Those in the highest echelon are Guild Masters.

##### Joining the Ninth Quill

The Ninth Quill operates in the shadows and therefore doesn't entertain applicants. However, the Guild Masters keep an eye on talented thieves and other morally ambiguous individuals with magical skills. If the Guild Masters believe such an individual would be an asset to the Ninth Quill, they extend an invitation to visit Blackscript College—through an anonymous courier, of course.

##### Blackscript College

The hidden academy where the Ninth Quill trains its members is located in a crime-ridden neighborhood of a busy trade city. Dilapidated warehouses, all owned by the Ninth Quill, surround the college to ensure its privacy. There's no readily apparent entrance; instead, it's accessible only by unmarked doors in twisting alleys and hidden passages.

The centuries-old college was once a legitimate magical academy, but city records show that it closed decades ago due to administrative corruption. In truth, the college simply went underground to teach magical spy craft and thievery.

A former instructor at Blackscript College's predecessor academy, the college's dean is the elf Sirethe Slipshadow (Medium Spy Master). Slipshadow is infamous for her ability to impersonate others and uses these skills to observe students during their studies and on early missions.

The college's library, called the Repository, holds an eclectic variety of stolen treasures. An adjacent chamber called the Ersatz Repository contains worktables where members make high-quality counterfeits of spellbooks, relics, and artifacts. Many cherished treasures held by other factions are actually Ninth Quill counterfeits.

### Phantasmic Circus

*[Image: Members of the Phantasmic Circus prepare for a magic-enhanced performance. (book/AU/054-04-012.phantasmic-circus-preparing.webp)]*

Part entertainers' alliance and part traveling carnival, the Phantasmic Circus is an organization of performers and illusionists who create grand spectacles and share magical techniques to delight, entertain, and misdirect.

#### Goals and Activities

The Phantasmic Circus stages elaborate performances across the world, moving from city to city to bring delight and wonder. These performances are huge extravaganzas involving dozens of entertainers and drawing large audiences. The circus is renowned for illusions and pyrotechnics that reach hundreds of feet into the air.

These complex routines change with every show; Phantasmic Circus performers are always experimenting with new illusions, death-defying stunts, and musical accompaniment. Tickets and concessions are pricey, but audience members remember the faction's thrilling spectacles for the rest of their lives.

The Phantasmic Circus's performances use trained creatures for certain acts, but audience members would be shocked to realize how few genuine creatures are on display. Lifelike illusions of animals and monsters perform most stunts, while living animals are treated carefully and kept out of danger.

##### One Circus, Many Troupes

The Phantasmic Circus isn't a single traveling troupe, but rather the collective name for several groups that operate independently, each under the guidance of a manager called a Wondermaker. Circus members might shift from group to group to hone their skills, so residents of a city might never realize that the "Phantasmic Circus" that visited twice the previous year was actually two different troupes.

#### Leadership and Organization

The astounding and popular Phantasmic Circus of today had humble beginnings as a small traveling carnival called the Hayver and Hayver Circus. When the Hayvers' youngest sibling, the human Della, returned to the family circus with training in Illusion magic and a gregarious gnome husband named Walnip Waddlebell, the circus started its meteoric rise.

Soon, Della and Walnip were running the circus as its ringmasters and self-appointed "First Maestros." The couple also gave the increasingly illusion-focused show a flashier name. The other Hayvers have since passed away, but the aging First Maestros (both Small Performer Legends), still plan spectacles, train new illusionists, and orchestrate the many troupes operating as the Phantasmic Circus.

##### Wondermakers

The First Maestros are now too old to tour, but they keep in touch with each troupe as its travels bring it back to the circus's headquarters, the Mirage Lyceum. The First Maestros appoint a Wondermaker to guide each troupe. Most Wondermakers are flashy showoffs or grand illusionists who enjoy the spotlight as ringmasters or star performers. In other troupes, the Wondermaker works behind the scenes, guiding the performers and pushing their shows to new heights.

Each Wondermaker keeps their troupe's books, protects its members, makes its travel plans, and coordinates with other Wondermakers to ensure they aren't revisiting cities too soon. They also send performers from troupe to troupe to teach gifted members additional skills or to resolve personality clashes.

##### Roustabouts

Although acrobats, illusionists, animals, and entertainers are the stars of the Phantasmic Circus, a platoon of supporters keeps the troupe running. Collectively called roustabouts, these laborers and guides let the performers take the glory while collecting reasonable pay from the troupe's Wondermaker. The visual artists who design the elaborate sets and costumes are also roustabouts; they mentor each other to hone their artistic skills.

### Seers of Sea and Sky

The Seers of Sea and Sky is a faction of oracles who make predictions about the future based on movements of the clouds and waves. Open to all methods of divination, the seers encourage communication with a wide array of aerial and undersea prophets and meticulously record their prognostications.

#### Goals and Activities

The Seers of Sea and Sky maintains libraries and reading rooms in many large, coastal cities. Within these hubs, seers cast Divination spells for reasonable costs; train other members in divination techniques; and maintain contact with visionaries across, above, and below the seas. Although obtaining accurate information through divination is important to the faction, sharing knowledge and imparting guidance to those most in need of it is also a crucial part of the faction's mission.

The seers primarily practice what they call hydromancy, or predictions based on watching the movement of water, and aeromancy, or predictions based on observing the weather. Members hone many subspecialties within these techniques. For example, some seers divine by scrutinizing driftwood delivered by ocean waves, while others base predictions on the movement of clouds.

##### Allies of Air and Water

Faction members live in diverse locales and include a wide array of folk. The faction's membership includes aarakocra, giant eagles, and other flying creatures who can provide detailed data about the wind and cloud movement. Other seers live beneath the waves, including bronze dragons and merfolk. Members from both groups and land-bound seers often convene, keeping the faction cohesive.

##### Truth Twice

The seers maintain a philosophy they call "truth twice." The faction doesn't consider a prophecy or revelation to be reliable unless it's been corroborated by another member—preferably one using a different Divination spell or method of divining. Checking and rechecking other members' work is routine. As a result, the seers' predictions have a reputation for reliability. The support members provide for each other contributes to the organization's close-knit culture.

*[Image: Members of the Seers of Sea and Sky perform powerful Divination magic outdoors to reveal terrible threats. (book/AU/055-04-013.seers-divining.webp)]*

#### Leadership and Organization

The oracles who founded the Seers of Sea and Sky were called the Scholars of Deep Truth. As the scholars' fame spread and wealthy patrons sought their guidance, the scholars built an exclusive, opulent lighthouse to entertain petitioners. The scholars worked with undersea oracles to strengthen the accuracy of their own prophecies.

When several misleading prognostications left locals victims of suspicious seaside disasters, the scholars started questioning their sources. They soon discovered an Aboleth named Naurishaul was manipulating them. Before the scholars could address this shocking news, a client exposed the scholars as minions of this malevolent undersea entity. While technically true, this announcement was misleading, as the scholars hadn't realized their answers came from such a sinister source.

##### A New Beginning

The scandal could have driven the scholars into hiding or ruin, but the organization publicly acknowledged its unsavory association and resolved to do better. Members established a network of mutual education and support, teaching genuine divination methods and rigorously double-checking their revelations. They kept the remote lighthouse as the faction's headquarters but established outposts to provide answers in seaside cities where petitioners of more limited means could seek guidance.

The efforts to rebuild the scholars' reputation worked. Experts in hydromancy and aeromancy joined the faction, attracted by its openness and support. Many were diviners from far-flung places, including windswept peaks and undersea communities. To reflect the organization's growing membership and shed its tainted legacy, the faction changed its name to the Seers of Sea and Sky.

Focused entirely on their divination mission, the seers eschew hierarchy. New members are expected to respect more senior members, but the faction in general has an egalitarian outlook. Beyond the Founders (see below), members consider titles and ranks unnecessary.

##### The Founders

The faction's inner circle consists of the original Scholars of Deep Truth members, though they use that scandal-ridden name only as a cautionary tale. Other seers simply refer to them as the Founders. With a reputation for humility, insight, and rigor, the Founders are highly regarded throughout the faction.

The Founders consist of the human siblings Kessalee Cloudseeker and Narvok Cloudseeker (Medium Mages), haughty elf Travieste Nilvinarian (Medium Noble Prodigy), and gregarious gnome Bulrug Seawhistle (Small Performer Maestro). The Founders frequently travel to consult with faction members scattered across land, sea, and air environments.

##### Wavewatchers

Some Seers of Sea and Sky have formed a coterie called the Wavewatchers. These members enjoy watching ocean waves crash against the shore, and they spend hours in contemplation, watching light play on the waves and listening to their soothing sounds. The Wavewatchers have created a meditation trend that attracts more faction members each week. A few of the newest faction members aren't diviners at all—they're mystics or monks who've joined to seek peace from the rolling waves.

However, some faction members have fallen behind in their work in favor of wave watching, and they're producing unconfirmed divinations. This is unacceptable to the Founders, who are displeased with the Wavewatchers phenomenon. Kessalee worries that their old foe Naurishaul has infiltrated the faction once again by mesmerizing susceptible faction members.

### Sheltering Hands

The Sheltering Hands is a benevolent organization of abjurers and advocates who operate emergency shelters and soup kitchens in far-flung settlements. This well-respected faction uses Abjuration magic to protect the vulnerable and remove afflictions.

#### Goals and Activities

Sheltering Hands members are skilled abjurers who focus on providing services to the needy. Faction members solicit donations of food, money, or land, then share them in directed ways to reduce as much suffering as possible. They operate shelters, aid disease-stricken regions, and work to lift curses.

Beyond fulfilling immediate needs, members of the Sheltering Hands help individuals rebuild their dignity, security, and self-determination. Its members happily share bread with the hungry, but they also delight in helping the hungry set up communal farms and other enterprises so they can feed themselves. Members also work against despots to build community solidarity and create support programs that help marginalized populations voice their needs.

Abjurers of the Sheltering Hands also teach the rudiments of defensive magic to those who show interest and aptitude. Faction members who are mages lift curses and remove debilitating conditions; even members who can't cast the appropriate spells usually know enough to recognize what spells are needed.

Many of those the faction helps become members themselves, seeking to pay forward the support they've received and prevent others from suffering as they did.

##### Balance of Faith

The Sheltering Hands isn't a religious organization, though some of its members are priests and many venerate deities of mercy, charity, and self-sacrifice. The faction doesn't favor any particular religion and welcomes good-hearted members of all sorts, including former followers of evil faiths who have renounced their earlier affiliations. This nonsectarian policy allows the Sheltering Hands to coordinate relief or rescue efforts across organizations.

##### Fieldwork

Instead of expecting those in need to journey to them, small teams of Sheltering Hands members travel to deliver food, refresh supplies, and cast Abjuration spells. Spells from other schools of magic are often useful to repair structures, produce a bounty from sickly crops, or chase away opportunistic predators, so teams often include spellcasters with specialties other than Abjuration.

### Evolving Magic Items

Few experiences infuse players with more wonder than their characters finding exciting magic items. Characters often grow fond of magic items that have personal significance, such as items that remind them of a beloved companion or a heroic deed.

As characters grow in power, though, that fondness can turn to frustration. A level 1 character's beloved Uncommon item might no longer be as useful by the time that character reaches level 5. Eventually, the character might have a dilemma: abandon their favorite magic item in favor of one with less personal significance, or keep the favored item, potentially forgoing a mechanically stronger option.

The magic item evolution rules in this section allow you to grant a magic item additional properties and increase its rarity as characters become more powerful. With these tools, you can build narrative significance into your characters' magic items and enable the items to grow in power as the characters do. This section presents several ready-made evolving magic items and rules for evolving certain magic items from the Dungeon Master's Guide or other sources.

#### When to Evolve Magic Items

You decide when a magic item evolves. In general, a magic item evolves when a character's power level increases to the next tier of play. Treat this as a guideline, not a rule.

##### Repeated Evolutions

You can evolve a magic item multiple times. Each time you evolve it, the magic item retains its properties from its previous rarity (or rarities) and gains a property from its new rarity.

You can evolve a magic item before or after awarding it to a character. In either case, you can increase the item's rarity as the character gains power until its rarity becomes Legendary.

##### Crafting Evolution

You might also decide that characters can evolve their magic items using the rules for crafting magic items in the Crafting Magic Items. If you allow this, the time required to evolve the item equals the difference between the time required to craft an item of the evolving item's new rarity and the time required to craft an item of its previous rarity. Likewise, the cost required is the difference between the cost to craft an item of the evolving item's new rarity and the cost to craft an item of its previous rarity. So evolving an Uncommon item (which requires 10 days and 200 GP to craft) into a Rare item (which requires 50 days and 2,000 GP to craft) requires 40 days and 1,800 GP in raw materials.

#### Results of Evolution

This section describes the results of magic item evolution.

##### Name and Appearance

Each item's name changes when it evolves, as noted for that type of evolving magic item. Its appearance also changes as it evolves.

##### Rarity

When a magic item evolves, its rarity increases to the next highest rarity: from Common to Uncommon, from Rare to Very Rare, and so forth. A Legendary magic item can't evolve.

##### Attunement

A magic item keeps its Attunement requirement (or lack thereof) as it evolves. If a creature is attuned to a magic item when the item evolves, the creature retains its Attunement to that item.

##### Cumulative Abilities

When an evolving magic item evolves, it retains the properties described in its previous rarities and gains the properties described in its new rarity. If an evolving weapon grants a bonus to attack rolls and damage rolls, however, that bonus replaces any similar bonus granted by a lower rarity instead of being added to it. For instance, in "Ready-Made Evolving Magic Items," a Keyholes Dagger's bonus to attack and damage rolls is +1 when Uncommon, +2 when Rare, and +3 when Very Rare.

#### Ready-Made Evolving Magic Items

The ready-made evolving magic items presented in this section and listed in the Ready-Made Evolving Magic Items table are special rewards. You might award a Ranger a Blossom Rod, for example, to reinforce the character's connection with nature, gradually increasing the rod's rarity to reward the Ranger for their heroics. Similarly, a Necromancer might receive a Staff of Skulls as a gift if their mentor works with the Covenant of the Grave magical faction (see x).

You can structure a campaign arc around one of these items—use it to point the characters toward adventures related to its narrative, similar to how you might use an Artifact.

### Adventure Background

The fiendish, immortal mage Venger weaves interlocking schemes across the D&D multiverse to amass power and thwart his enemies. Some of Venger's schemes involve allying with—or opposing—heroes he manipulates to his own ends.

To identify heroes who might prove useful in future plots, Venger has built tombs across the multiverse as testing grounds. When heroes' deeds catch his attention, he invites those heroes to claim the treasure inside the tombs. Venger often challenges heroes to explore numerous tombs before he's convinced of their usefulness.

The offer of treasure is real—though Venger considers the treasures in his tombs inconsequential, adventurers might find them worth the risk. But the tombs are deadly, and they've become the final resting place for many adventurers who've failed Venger's challenges.

Among the earliest tombs Venger built is the Tomb of the Shadow Serpents. It's situated within a cave on the Material Plane near an intersection with the Shadowfell, so strange and gloomy environs envelop the tomb. The tomb holds deadly monsters and traps. Surviving the Tomb of the Shadow Serpents is the first step toward earning Venger's respect.

When the characters are ready to venture into the cave described in "Reaching the Tomb," read or paraphrase the following:

> Three hulking, wolflike beasts lumber around this wide cave entrance, their red, malevolent eyes peering at you. Gray light flickering from sconces reflects off an obsidian mosaic in a double door at the back of the cave entrance. The mosaic depicts an S-shaped serpent.

Three Worgs lurk here and begin combat when the characters move 10 feet into the cave.

The worgs recently adopted the cave entrance as their home, assuming that creatures living inside the double door would periodically emerge and provide the worgs with scrumptious meals. The characters are the worgs' first potential victims, and the hungry creatures fight to the death. The worgs don't know about Venger or care about the purpose of the tomb.

###### Serpent Mosaic

The obsidian serpent mosaic on the double door is one foot wide and one foot tall. It's identical to the mosaic the characters must reconstruct to open the serpent puzzle door in area x.

##### T2: Tomb Entryway

> A stone double door stands in the east wall of this chamber, and another stands in the west wall. Flanking the path that leads deeper into the temple are two identical statues. Each is seven feet tall and depicts a one-horned, winged man wearing robes and an ominous grin.

These statues depict x, as any character familiar with the mage easily recognizes. Any character unfamiliar with Venger can take the Study action to examine a statue and make a DC 10 Intelligence (History or Religion) check to try to identify the statue's likeness.

###### Statue Traps

The statues are magical traps. The first time a creature moves within 5 feet of a statue, that statue intones in a deep, commanding voice, "Clasp my hand for a boon."

If a creature touches a statue's hand, a fiery jolt of energy discharges and the creature makes a DC 12 Dexterity saving throw, taking 5 (1d10) Fire damage on a failed save or half as much damage on a successful one. On a success or failure, the statue's magical voice chuckles and shouts, "Expect no favors!" Each statue activates only once.

###### Detecting the Traps

A Detect Magic spell reveals an aura of Evocation magic around each statue. As a Search action, a creature within 5 feet of a statue can examine it and make a DC 10 Wisdom (Perception) check, noticing that the statue's hand emits warmth. The traps can't be disarmed, but they can be avoided by simply not touching the statues' hands.

##### T3: Deadly Corridor

> A desiccated corpse wearing rusty armor lies sprawled on this corridor's floor. The circlet on the corpse's head is strangely pristine and glints in the sconces' light, and a serpent-shaped blade protrudes from the body. Double doors lead east, west, and south.

This T-shaped corridor leads deeper into the tomb.

###### Blade Traps

There are two pressure plates in this corridor, as denoted on the map. When a creature moves onto a pressure plate for the first time on a turn or starts its turn there, a gap opens in the adjacent wall, and a serpent-shaped blade flies toward the creature. The creature on the plate must succeed on a DC 15 Dexterity saving throw or take 3 (1d6) Slashing damage. Each trap activates once per turn. Once a trap has activated three times, the trap ceases to function.

###### Detecting and Disarming the Traps

As a Search action, a creature can examine a section of floor that forms a pressure plate and make a DC 15 Wisdom (Perception) check, detecting the pressure plate on a successful check. Wedging an Iron Spike or similar object under the pressure plate prevents the trap from triggering.

###### Fallen Adventurer

Except for the circlet, the fallen adventurer's equipment is dented and unusable.

###### Treasure

The circlet on the corpse's head is a Circlet of Blasting.

##### T4: Mud Pit

> Spanning most of this chamber is a large pit of bubbling mud, from which rise shimmering waves of heat. A rickety bridge made of chains and decaying wooden planks crosses the pit.

The chain bridge is Difficult Terrain. When a creature steps on the bridge for the first time on a turn or starts its turn there, it must succeed on a DC 12 Dexterity saving throw or fall into the mud pit. A creature makes this save only once per turn.

###### Mud Pit

The area of the mud pit acts like a 10-foot-deep quicksand pit hazard (see the Dungeon Master's Guide), but the mud is also dangerously hot thanks to a natural underground hot spring. Any creature that starts its turn in the mud takes 3 (1d6) Fire damage.

###### Treasure

The western alcove is full of detritus covered in dried mud. A thorough search of the alcove takes 1 minute and yields a Potion of Fire Breath.

##### T5: Spiderling Den

> The air in this dark, enormous cave is cool and damp. Thick spiderwebs cover the center of the floor and the entrance to a tunnel leading away to the east. A lumpy, five-foot-wide sac of webbing bulges from the eastern wall next to the tunnel's entrance.

Six juvenile giant spiders (use the Giant Wolf Spider stat block) lurk within the webs in the center of this chamber. They're the young of the giant spiders in area T10. A character who takes a Search action to survey the webs and succeeds on a DC 17 Wisdom (Perception) check notices the spiders.

As soon as a character ventures within 10 feet of the webs, the spiders emerge and begin combat. They fight to the death.

###### Webs

Areas covered in webs, as denoted on the map, use the rules for the webs hazard (see Webs).

###### Egg Sac

The sac on the eastern wall holds unhatched spider eggs. The sac has AC 10; HP 8; Vulnerability to Fire damage; and Immunity to Piercing, Poison, and Psychic damage. The sac is unaffected if the webs around it are destroyed.

Ten minutes after a character first enters this area, the eggs hatch and four Spiders emerge. The spiders fight the characters on sight.

##### T6: Long Corridor

> On this corridor's floor slump three corpses with darts protruding from their flesh. The center body clutches a shiny, black object in one hand.

Bloodstains splatter the floor of this hallway.

###### Dart Traps

There are three pressure plates in this corridor, as denoted on x. Each pressure plate triggers a poisoned darts trap (see the Dungeon Master's Guide).

###### Fallen Adventurers

The adventurers' equipment is dented and unusable.

###### Serpent Puzzle Piece

The body in the center of the corridor clutches a piece of the serpent mosaic in area x. When the characters find this piece, show them the Handout: Serpent Puzzle Piece 1.

##### T7: Hall of Armor

> Resting on a dais at the west end of this chamber are two suits of shining plate armor. Each suit is arranged on a stand whose wooden base is adorned with silver filigree. A few stairs lead up to the dais.

Both suits are Animated Armor. As soon as a character ventures within 5 feet of the stairs, the suits begin combat.

When a character is within 5 feet of an animated armor, they notice a seal etched on the armor's chest that depicts the ominously grinning face of a one-horned fiend—identical to the faces of the statues of Venger in area x.

###### Treasure

The wooden base of each armor stand includes a small drawer. The drawers are locked. As a Utilize action, a character can try to pick the lock using Thieves' Tools, doing so with a successful DC 15 Dexterity (Sleight of Hand) check.

The drawer in the south stand holds two obsidian stones worth 10 GP each. The drawer in the north stand holds a Bag of Tricks (tan).

##### T8: Lacedons' Lair

> This wide cavern's walls are slick with trickling water that forms a murky, ten-foot-wide pool. To the east of the pool lies a one-armed corpse, next to which glints a shiny, black object. Strange gurgles emanate from the cavern's western end.

Two Lacedon Ghouls lurk in this cavern. One is around a corner in the western alcove, chewing on an arm it severed from the body in the northeast corner. Another is underwater in the pool, waiting to ambush trespassers.

As soon as a character ventures within 5 feet of the fallen adventurer or comes within 25 feet of the western wall, the underwater ghoul jumps out of the pool and fights them. The sounds of combat beginning alert the ghoul in the western alcove; that ghoul drops the arm it's chewing on and joins the fight.

###### Fallen Adventurer

The ghouls killed an adventurer and have been snacking on the corpse, which is covered in bite marks. The adventurer's equipment is dented and unusable.

###### Murky Pool

The pool is 5 feet deep and filled with cool, mineral-laden water.

###### Serpent Puzzle Piece

Next to the body is a piece of the serpent mosaic in area T11. When the characters find this piece, show them the Handout: Serpent Puzzle Piece 2.

##### T9: Webbed Tunnel

> Thick spiderwebs cover the floor, walls, and ceiling of this tunnel.

The area of this tunnel is a webs hazard (see the Hazards).

##### T10: Spiders' Nest

> Thick webs spill from this long cave's western opening. Perched on gossamer mounds of webbing to the north and south are two enormous spiders with hairy legs. On the northeast wall, a severed hand suspended in the webs holds a shiny, black object.

There are two Giant Spiders here: one in the northeast corner and one in the southeast alcove. As soon as the characters enter this cave, the spiders fight them.

The spiders are hungry and fight to the death. They long ago ate the body the severed hand came from.

###### Serpent Puzzle Piece

The severed hand holds a piece of the serpent mosaic in area T11. When the characters find this piece, show them the Handout: Serpent Puzzle Piece 3.

##### T11: Serpent Puzzle Door

> The stone double door at the west end of this hall bears a mosaic similar to the one outside the tomb. But the space that held an obsidian, S-shaped serpent in the entrance's mosaic is empty in this one. Softly glowing blue lines illuminate the serpent-shaped void.

This door's magical lock is linked to the mosaic. The glowing lines in the mosaic's empty spaces thrum with magic.

###### Serpent Mosaic

The mosaic is one foot wide and one foot tall. Placing the three missing pieces of the mosaic's obsidian serpent in the mosaic unlocks the door. The pieces are located in the long corridor (area x), the lacedons' lair (area x), and the spiders' nest (area x).

Alternatively, a character can take a Magic action to trace the glowing lines and simulate the puzzle pieces' correct placement. A character who does so makes a DC 18 Intelligence (Arcana) check. On a successful check, the door unlocks.

##### T12: The Shadowy Sepulcher

*[Image: The shadows that haunt the sepulcher were once heroes who betrayed Venger. (book/AU/087-07-007.shadows.webp)]*

> Before you stands an eight-foot-tall central statue, around which swirl tendrils of wispy, black shadow. The statue is made of faceted obsidian and depicts entwined serpents whose heads rise toward the ceiling. On a dais to the west are three sarcophagi, their marble lids carved with the likenesses of warriors in repose.

Three Shadows lurk in this room, each hidden in one of the sarcophagi. As soon as a character ventures west of the serpent statue, the shadows emerge and begin combat.

The shadows arose from the bodies of adventurers Venger killed for betraying him. The mage then bound the shadows to this room. In a cruel act of irony, Venger built elaborate sarcophagi for the adventurers' bodies and placed them here, taunting the shadows with the prospect of eternal rest.

###### Serpent Statue

Venger built the serpent statue and imbued it with magic he drew from the Shadowfell. As a result, the statue causes this room to behave as if the room were on that plane. It also causes the shadows to take on a serpentine appearance.

A character who enters this room must succeed on a DC 10 Wisdom saving throw or be affected by despair. Roll on the Shadowfell Despair table in the Dungeon Master's Guide to determine the form the despair takes.

The statue has AC 15, HP 18, and Vulnerability to Radiant damage. If the statue is reduced to 0 HP, the Shadowfell stops affecting this room, and any despair effects on the characters end immediately.

A character who takes the Study action to examine the statue and succeeds on a DC 15 Intelligence (Arcana) check understands how the statue is affecting the room and surmises that its connection to the Shadowfell gives it Vulnerability to Radiant damage.

###### Treasure

The sarcophagus lids are made of solid marble. A character can take a Utilize action to make a DC 12 Strength (Athletics) check, lifting one of the lids on a successful check.

Inside each sarcophagus are the desiccated remains of an adventurer, whose equipment is rusted and unusable. The north sarcophagus also holds a bloodstone worth 50 GP. The central sarcophagus holds a Cube of Summoning. The south sarcophagus holds three turquoises worth 10 GP apiece.

### Conclusion

Once the characters are finished exploring the Tomb of the Shadow Serpents, award each character 100 XP for completing the adventure. The characters should have enough XP to advance to level 2 by the end of the adventure. If you're using a method of level advancement that doesn't rely on XP, the characters gain a level after defeating the shadows in the sepulcher.

If the characters survive the Tomb of the Shadow Serpents, Venger notices their prowess. He tracks the characters throughout their adventuring careers and might challenge them to explore other tombs he's created throughout the multiverse.

Dungeons in several published D&D adventures can be easily repurposed as tombs that Venger challenges the characters to explore. The temple in the level 4 adventure "The Will of Orcus" in 1, for example, could be reimagined as such a tomb, with the cultists of the demon lord Orcus instead becoming sycophantic followers of Venger. The Tomb of the Nine Gods in 7, meant for level 9 adventurers, could similarly be reimagined as a complex created by Venger instead of the lich Acererak. The Tomb of Wayward Souls in 7, another dungeon created by Acererak, is appropriate for level 16 characters. You can also create new tombs that Venger challenges the characters to explore.

At your discretion, after the characters have survived the dangers of numerous tombs, Venger might offer the characters mysterious jobs such as finding an item, stopping or delivering a shipment of goods, or protecting a public figure. These schemes should feed into Venger's attempts to thwart the League of Malevolence, an odious assemblage of power-hungry villains led by Venger's former lackey, the human mage Kelek. The characters shouldn't trust Venger, but they might accept his jobs (which always pay well) out of curiosity or as part of a plan to double-cross him.
